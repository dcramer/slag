# Game design decisions

This is a concise log of decided rules. It's the source of truth for gameplay intent. When a decision changes, update this file in the same commit. Implementation details live in AGENTS.md and the code. The full log of the owner's decisions, with dates, status and what each one replaced, is [DECISIONS.md](DECISIONS.md).

## Lore
Design guidance, never game text (see Discovery). When a choice is unclear, ask what fits this world.

> **Slag** is what's left over. Every game anyone loves is poured from the Great Foundry, and the runoff cools into Slag: a scrap world of melted-down levels (a farm here, a castle there, half a space station) and the characters who never made the cut, from stray animals to knock-off heroes who almost got their own game.
>
> Once a night the Foundry opens a single mould. Whoever reaches the flag gets cast into the next real game. Everyone builds the course together from whatever scrap tumbles out of the crate, and everyone sabotages it, because there's only one mould.

What it explains: themes are melted-down levels (game-like places, slightly off); the cast is rejects and knock-offs, so parody belongs; parts are scrap from other games; one mould means one winner, and a course everyone finishes earns nobody the mould.

## Core loop
- Lobby → **Pick** (claim one prop from a crate) → **Place** (put it in the level) → **Race** to the flag → **Score**. Repeat until someone reaches the target score or the round limit, whichever comes first, then **Victory**.
- Matches have 1–8 players. Players can be local (any mix of keyboard, gamepads and touch) or online.
- Props persist for the whole match, so levels get more dangerous every round.
- Round 1 is often unfinishable, especially with few players. That's expected: nearly every level needs parts, and parts build up over the match.

## Discovery
- Part of the joy of the game is the unknown. Players learn parts, rules and tricks by playing and by watching each other.
- The game never explains the obvious: no sub lines, hints or notes that restate what's on screen or spell out a rule. If the screen can show it, don't say it. The main menu's short sub lines are the one exception, kept as part of the home screen's look.

## Parody
- Our goal is to parody other games, especially with items. Players should recognize what's being mocked and laugh at the twist.
- What we parody: the build-then-race party platformer is the core loop's inspiration. Nintendo's platformers are the main well: homages across many franchises, the plumber's blocks, pipes and shells, the ape's barrels and girders, and more. The big crossover brawler is the model for items and stage hazards: lots of them, chaotic and readable. Past those, any iconic pop-culture game is fair game, as the team shooter's sentry gun was; [PARODY-BACKLOG.md](PARODY-BACKLOG.md) ranks the candidates.
- A parody is ours: our own name, our own art and a comic twist on the idea. Never the original's real name, art, characters or level designs.
- Mock the trope before any one game; a nod at one game gets a sound-alike or spoof name. Each part's target, silhouette and visual gag are in [PARODY.md](PARODY.md).
- Props are objects, not characters. No item, part, projectile or set piece has a face or a persona (eyes, a mouth, an expression, little feet, a cap worn like a character); only runners have faces. The parody comes through the object's shape, material and mechanism.
- **Player characters may parody famous characters**: a mustached plumber, a jellyfish-brained floater, a hard-hat engineer, a goose up to no good. Each is our own design under a sound-alike name, recognisable at a glance as the homage, never the real character, its name or its art. Parts and machines stay faceless.

## Scoring
- Finishing earns +10 points. The first player to finish gets an extra +4.
- If you finish, each rival killed by your prop, or by the level right after your bonk, earns you +2. Traps alone don't pay: you have to reach the flag too.
- If you finish while trailing the leader, after 2 or more rounds in a row without a point, you earn an underdog bonus of +3.
- If everyone finishes, nobody scores ("too easy"). If nobody finishes, nobody scores either.
- Solo play just rewards finishing.
- A match always ends: scoreless rounds can run forever, so the round limit has no "off" setting.
- The lobby sets both win conditions with one **Match Length**: Short (30 points or 10 rounds), Normal (50 points or 15 rounds, the default) or Long (80 points or 25 rounds). The limit is meant to decide some matches, mostly with 2 players, where a round only scores if exactly one player finishes.
- At the end of a match the top score wins and ties are joint winners. If the round limit hits with nobody on the board, there is no winner.
- Anything that lists scores shows the most points first, with ties in slot order. This is display only: player order in the sim never changes. On the score screen every row is a lane racing to one finish line at the target score: this round's awards drop in as blocks, one reason at a time, and the rows slide to their new rank as they land.

## Players
- Characters are generated per player (kind, colors, face and name) and are **cosmetic only**: every player has the same hitbox and the same physics.
- **The cast is mostly homages**: ten famous game characters in our own designs (a mustached plumber, the blue speedster, a green-tunic elf, an armored bounty hunter, a yellow electric mouse, an impostor crewmate, a pink puffball, a jellyfish parasite, a goose up to no good, a hard-hat engineer) and four plain animals (fox, frog, penguin, chicken). **Recognisability comes first**: each homage keeps the iconic palette and the three or four traits people know it by, so a stranger names it at a glance. **The player's color** is the body for a plain animal or a homage that comes in every color anyway (the crewmate, the puffball); on a homage in its own palette it's a flat ring at its feet. Either way it matches the HUD. Each kind is its own design with its own outline, props and gait. A lobby never seats two of the same kind. New Look rolls everything, the kind included; there's no picker. Drawn clean and light: a thin, varied pen in a dark of the body's own color. Details: [CHARACTERS.md](CHARACTERS.md).
- The local player's **name and look persist** across sessions (localStorage). On first join the game asks for a name, with the random one offered as the one-click default. New Look rerolls the look and keeps the name.
- **Everyone in a lobby looks different**: no two players share a kind, a name or a color, and their colors sit well apart.
- **Spawn zone is safe**: the starting zone is the protected rect around the spawn (the start pocket), shown during races as a faint shimmer. While your body touches it, props and items can't hurt you: hazards don't kill, projectiles that reach you pop harmlessly, bonks and smoke don't reach you, and moving solids can't crush you. A faint bubble marks protected runners. The pit, giving up and the race timer still apply (the pit can't be reached from inside the zone anyway).
- **Players are solid.** A head is a one-way platform: land on it, stand on it, ride it as it moves, jump off it, drop through it with down + jump. It counts as ground for coyote time and jump buffering. Whoever is underneath carries whoever is on top, a rising runner lifts them, and a ceiling stops the stack (the jumper bonks, nobody is squeezed). Stacking to reach higher is a feature, and the generator never counts on it: levels stay finishable alone. Being stood on never hurts.
- **Side contact blocks and shoves.** Walking into someone standing still moves you both at about a quarter of walking speed (a run-up shoves harder). Equal efforts stall, a stronger push wins by the difference, and being rear-ended never slows you down. Nobody is ever pushed into terrain. A hazard you're shoved or carried into kills as usual, credited the usual way: the shover gets nothing. No wall slides or wall jumps off people.
- **Nobody can hold a line.** Pressed against bodies for 0.75 s, you slip through them, so a one-tile tunnel or bridge can't be body-blocked and runners meeting head-on in a corridor both get through. Runners that already overlap (leaving the spawn pile, a pipe exit, squeezed together by a moving block) pass through each other until they've come apart, with no pop.
- **Nobody waits on someone who isn't there.** A player with no input at all (no button, no pointer moving) for 30 s of a pick, a place or a race isn't waited for: they're handed a card from the crate, their part is skipped, or their runner gives up. Time before the phase began doesn't count, so a finished runner can watch the rest of a race. Once that happens they're **AFK** until their next input: handed a card at once, skipped, popped at GO, like a disconnected player. In the lobby it takes 60 s, and an AFK player doesn't hold up everyone else's Ready (somebody present still has to ready). Their card says AFK. Score moves on by itself, and anyone can continue from Victory, so neither waits on anyone. Online, an AFK player is moved to the watchers (below): at once in the lobby, and mid-match once two rounds in a row have ended with them AFK, their seat freeing at the boundary. Any input asks for the seat back.
- **Who touches whom.** Runners inside the spawn zone don't touch anyone, so a pile can't block the start. Finished and dead runners touch nobody, and neither does anyone a rope or keg is holding. Frozen and dazed runners stay solid: you can stand on them and shove them. A stack on a glider weighs it down like that many riders.

## Movement (precision-platformer feel)
- **Walk vs run.** Walking is slow and precise, about 6.6 tiles/s, with short jumps of about 4 tiles. Holding **Run** gives about 13.4 tiles/s and jumps of about 8 tiles, but run momentum builds over a beat, skids when you let go or turn, and carries you past ledges if you're careless.
- A full jump is about 3.7 tiles high and a tap about 1.3. Variable jump height (hold to go higher), coyote time, jump buffering, a little hang at the apex, and fast-fall (hold down in the air).
- **Wall slide and wall jump.** Holding into the wall while mashing jump climbs it; holding away kicks off hard.
- **One-way platforms**: jump up through them; down + jump drops through.
- Forgiving corner correction on ceilings and ledge lips.
- **Gravity is a level setting**: Auto (rolled per level), Low, Normal or High. Levels are validated and spaced at their own gravity: under low gravity even the easy gaps open wider, so a floaty jump still has something to cross. On Auto, space has its own gravity, floatier than Low (0.55 of normal: a full jump is about 6.6 tiles high and a second in the air); a host's Low, Normal or High still wins there. Runners and everything in flight fall at the level's gravity.

## Levels
- Every level has a **theme**: farm, factory, castle, jungle, volcano, harbor, mine, rooftops, glacier or space. The theme drives the terrain shapes, look, music and set pieces, so a farm reads as a farm. Glacier and space each come up on about 8% of seeds, the other eight on about 10.5% each; adding them moved no seed between the original eight.
- **A theme may change how you move, through the shared pieces only.** Its levels are still proven solvable with the real controller on that footing and at that gravity.
  - **Glacier** lays ice into its own terrain: the same surface an ice coat gives, so ice means one thing everywhere: long skids, a faster top speed once you build it, and a face you can't wall-jump. It goes in the beds, the long stretches of floor, and stops two tiles of rock short of any edge it drops off, so a skid ends on rock and not in a crevasse. The banks of a crossing that needs parts, the pockets, and anything too short to skate on stay rock; seracs, frozen falls and ice-cave roofs are ice right through. Ice is drawn as clear ice, so you can always tell it from rock, and no weather is drawn over it; rain or snow makes it slicker still, as with every slick surface. Any snow you see on a glacier is real snow weather.
  - **Space** floats: no ground and no floor to the pit, only docks, asteroids or station modules over the void, and falling off is death. Every gap is sized from jumps at space's gravity, so the voids are wide and the bodies small and few: the gaps between them are the long end of a walking jump, and a host's Normal or High packs them back in. That leaves no room for towers, so space levels are classic or wide. A classic space level asks for one part and a wide one more often for two. There's no weather in space.
- **Levels vary**: size and aspect (wide, classic, tall), sparse vs dense terrain, few vs many hazards, and route shape (sprawl, climb, descent, zig-zag).
- **Levels are mostly open.** Corridors are fine in places, but there's never only one narrow way to the goal: there are always route options, with hazards and choices along them. Measured on the real controller's routes with a level's required parts in place: take the fastest route and widen it by 2 tiles either side; a second route must stay out of that lane for at least 40% of the way. The spawn and goal pockets and the span of a bridge don't count, since nobody has a choice there. A level short of that gets raised lanes of jump-through platforms (carved galleries in a mine), and towers get two connectors between every pair of storeys. A tower's parts go on moats every route has to cross, on the goal storey and, for a second part, the spawn storey.
- **Run is a choice.** Most gaps can be walked across and some take a run-up; platforms too short to run on are always a walking hop apart.
- **Levels need parts.** About 99% can't be finished bare, and 20–30% need two parts; the rest need one. What stops a bare run is a gap or a wall a part solves, never a sealed level. Every level must be finishable with **at most two small bridges**, verified with the real movement controller and never with hand-waved distances.
- The spawn and goal zones are protected: nothing can be built or bombed there.
- **The flag is generous**: its finish trigger is a bit wider than the flag and reaches 4 tiles up, so jumping clear over it still counts. Touching it ends your control: you drop, land and ride platforms with normal physics, and nothing can hurt you anymore.
- **Weather** is a per-level random variable, one kind per level, set by the seed and weighted per theme. About 75% of levels stay clear. Mines only ever get mist, rain and snow stay out of volcanoes and factories, and space never has any. Nothing on screen says what it does: you read it off the world.
  - **Anything you can see of weather does something, and nothing cosmetic pretends to be weather.** No theme or mood draws rain, snow, fog, falling ash or blowing leaves, and banners, grass, vines and flags hang still unless a gust is really coming or blowing.
  - **Wind**: level-wide gusts on a beat that's fixed per level, blowing one way for the whole level. They push runners on the ground and in the air, gliders, and every shot in flight. A gust beats a walk and loses to a run: stroll into one and you go backwards, and to stand your ground you run. A warning always runs first: 1.2 seconds of thickening wind lines, leaning grass and banners and a rising whistle, then the push. Between gusts the air is still and silent, and there's always more calm than the longest move the solvability proof uses. You can see the gusts come and go while you pick and place.
  - **Mist** limits sight: everyone has the same clear air around them, and there's a smaller clearing at the flag, so you know where to go but not what's in the way. Every live runner gets it, on every screen, so nobody sees further than anyone else. It's a thin veil while you pick and place, and closes in over the countdown. It's render only.
  - **Rain** keeps your speed and takes your brakes: long skids. **Snow** is a little slower and about twice as far to stop. Both are on everything you can stand on: terrain, one-ways, placed blocks, bridges, moving platforms, set pieces and gliders. Slick things get slicker, conveyors still run and jelly still bounces, and walls keep working. Wet ground shines, snow lies on every top, and it only falls when the footing is real.
  - **Weather never changes what a level needs.** It's chosen after the terrain and from its own roll, so the terrain, the solution and the share of levels needing parts are the same with or without it. The real controller then checks each pick: rain or snow has to leave the solution working and the level mostly open and still in need of its parts, and a gust can't do a part's job, even with the player choosing when to use it. A tailwind that would turns into a headwind, and a level that fails anything else stays clear.
- **The picture fills the window.** The play view keeps one aspect and scale, so nobody sees more of a level by resizing, and there are no bars or visible edge around it. The play view and any level shorter than it sit centered, so spare height goes evenly above and below. The sky and skyline run to the window's edges, and past the map's edge the level carries on as hazy scenery in its own theme: farmland, curtain walls, a harbor shed and open water, solid rock, moraine slopes, or, in space, bodies and debris adrift over the void. Below the map the pool carries on down, and whatever stands on the bottom of the level runs on under its surface, so the pit still reads as death; in space nothing stands on anything, so nothing runs on. None of the scenery shows inside the play area. The scenery can't be reached, and corner UI stays in the window's corners.
- **The camera always frames every live runner**, local or remote, with a margin: it zooms out as far as it takes, down to the whole level, and catches up instantly if smoothing would let anyone leave the screen. Finished and dead runners drop out of the framing.
- **Cel shading is the art direction**: flat tones, hard light bands, ink outlines, hard shadows. It is the one look for everyone, not a setting.
- **Render layers, back to front:** sky and skyline; background halves of set pieces (a windmill's tower, a crane's mast); liquids (water and lava); terrain, props and the moving parts of set pieces (the windmill's blades); players; effects. Scenery that you can't touch sits behind the terrain.
- Set pieces are large, pre-placed theme props: tractor, windmill, foundry press, chandelier, minecart, a mine roof that caves in and a runaway cart with its bell ringing, geysers, crane, a frost cannon and a chairlift on the glacier, a haunted manor's broken windows bristling with glass, solar flares licking up out of the void and a centrifuge in space, and so on. They never block the known solution and keep their hazards away from spawn and goal. They are visible in every phase.

### Stage concepts
A level is a place with one landmark you'd name it after: the Great Wheel, the Haunted Wing (docs/SETPIECES.md has the plan and every concept).
- **About 80% of levels get a concept and about 20% stay plain**, where a concept fits the level's size and route. The concept is rolled once per level from its own stream. Plain levels are generated exactly as before concepts existed.
- **The landmark comes first.** The sculptor takes the landmark's site and builds its structure as tiles, so the real controller proves routes over it like any terrain. Its moving parts and hazards go in after weather, every one of them required; the filler set pieces after them come from the concept's own hazard family. A landmark that won't stand up is retried, and after 8 tries the level goes plain.
- **A level with a landmark is named after it**, for example "The Great Wheel of Skookaish Mill". Plain levels keep their place name.
- **Movers are never required.** A lift, a wheel or a cart is a fast or risky way, never the only one: the proof stays static, and every concept keeps a static way past itself.
- **Except a carrier, and then it's big** (decision 1a). A giant contraption may carry you over a gap no part could close: a lift or ferry parked at each end, across a chasm wider (or a climb taller) than two parts can manage at the level's gravity, and the contraption at least as big as the gap. It's proven conservatively: a runner who steps on while it's parked at one end and stands still is carried to the other, with nothing in the way, and steps off while it's parked there. Anything that check can't prove keeps a static way past. The carrier's gap isn't the level's part-requiring crossing: the level still needs its one or two parts elsewhere, and no mover, the carrier included, may let a bare run skip that crossing.
- **Every theme has at least 5 landmarks**, all large (a third of a level or more), and between one and three of them grand (they own most of the level, or it's built inside them). Every landmark varies with its seed: storey count, widths, where the doors, stairs and movers go, which hazards it carries, so no two Haunted Wings are the same building.
- **Every theme also has at least 12 decor pieces** (scenery that dresses the level: theme-specific, grounded, varied by seed, never in the way) **and at least 3 hazards unique to it** (world-only set-piece hazards no other theme uses, on top of the shared ones).
- **A landmark has more than one way through, and blocking one is play.** Every concept builds at least two genuinely different routes past itself (the Haunted Wing's stairs, hall and lift), with doorways and corridors a placed beam or block can shut. Shutting one to force rivals the long way is intended strategy. The winnability plan still refuses the placement that would shut the last way.
- Triggers between pieces (a button that calls the lift) and changes between rounds are out of scope for now.

## Props
- **Not everything is death.** Most parts have an upside *and* a side effect you have to worry about: knockback, slow, freeze, sticky, misdirection, or a platform that sinks. Lethal parts exist, but disruption is the norm.
- **Some projectiles are platforms.** For example, gliders fired from a launcher can be ridden, but they sag and sink under weight or over time.
- **Demolition clears parts; changing terrain is rare.** Bombs, ring and cross charges, time bombs and EMPs destroy placed parts and leave the level's terrain alone. Only the drill digs terrain, plus a rare small mining bomb (one bomb in five). All respect spawn and goal protection.
- **The loot box** is one part with one look: a solid one-tile question-block cube. Hit it from below or bump its side and a random *held item* pops out of the top (out of the bottom under a low roof); any item can come out. Standing on it does nothing. A box either refills on a timer or runs out and goes dark. Which item pops is seeded per race, so a crate that survives a round doesn't repeat itself, and it's the same on every peer.
- **Held items** are carried one at a time, grabbed by touching them (contested items go to the nearest runner). The Use button fires them; down + Use tosses them. Loose items fizzle out after 10 s. Runners who die or finish drop theirs, except into the pit. Nothing carries over between rounds. **Items never kill on their own**: they bonk (a shove plus ~0.5 s of seeing stars, with no control), and a death soon after a bonk credits the bonker, like any trap kill. Every item has a catch:
  - **Master Noodle** (4 swings, a foam sword): bonks rivals and swats shots back, which makes them yours (rideable gliders and stuck patches are left alone). You crawl while you swing.
  - **Evicted Shell** (a kicked shell): slides along floors and banks off walls, and can come back to bonk its kicker.
  - **Stunnerang** (a boomerang): bonks the first rival it meets and curves back to your hand, wherever you've run to. If it hits anything else it clatters down for anyone to grab. A launcher's boomerang stays lethal.
  - **Bubble Wrap** (a pop shield): soaks one lethal hit and pops you upward, with a short guard after. It doesn't stop falls or crushes, and carrying it lowers your jumps. Use drops it.
  - **Free-Trial Jetpack**: one huge steerable burst upward, then the fumes daze you.
  - **Fog of War** (2 smoke bombs): a thick cloud that hides the ground and slows everyone inside, you included.
  - **Walk-Off Bat** (the brawler's home-run bat, 1 swing): Use winds up, and you're rooted until it lets go: press again to swing early, or it swings by itself at full charge (about 0.6 s). A tap knocks less than the noodle, a full charge about three times as hard. It swats shots like the noodle. A bonk mid wind-up throws the charge away, though you keep the bat.
  - **Tremor Brick** (the block that shakes the floor, 1 use): raised overhead for a beat, then slammed: every rival standing on something is bonked and bucked up. Anyone in the air is untouched, and so is anyone in the spawn zone or held by a part.
  - **Front-Runner Tax** (the leader-hunting winged shell, 1 use): flies over everything to whoever is nearest the flag by real route (not a straight line), hovers over them for a second with its shadow on the ground, then dives on the spot they stood and bonks everyone within two tiles. It retargets if the leader changes before the dive. If you lead, it comes for you. A dive into the spawn zone just pops.
  - **Pocket Block** (3 blocks): puts one solid turf block under your leading foot, ahead of you, or right under you in the air. It lasts 4 s, shakes, then crumbles. It never goes in a protected zone, on terrain or a part, or in a cell with anyone in it. Items may make solids mid-race like this; the solvability proof never counts on them.
- **Grab props** hold you and move your body in place of the controller; your hitbox doesn't change. A part holds one runner at a time, and a contested grab goes to the nearest runner, then the lower slot. There's a short cooldown before you can re-grab the part you just left, but any other part is fair game at once, so they chain. While held, fields, springs, wind and kickers skip you and other runners pass through you. Hazards, the pit, the flag and self-destruct all still apply. If the part goes away, a solid moves into you, or anything shoves you (a knock, a shield pop, a rocket), it lets go and you keep your speed.
  - **Sausage Vine** (the jungle-vine swing, on a butcher's hook): hangs from a ceiling or a placed block. Where there's no ceiling at all above it, it's lowered in on a string from open sky, so it can always be placed; it snaps a few rows up or down to reach a ceiling, and rotation does nothing. It falls with the ceiling it hangs from. You grab it automatically when you touch it in the air, wherever you touch it, and your speed becomes swing speed. Left and right pump the swing; jump lets go along the swing with a little extra fling, enough that a good release beats a running jump. **The catch:** it snaps if you hang on too long and grows back later. The links sear and thin first. Held items work on it.
  - **Kegapult** (the barrel cannon, as a moonshine keg): pulls you in on contact, on foot or in the air. Some hold a fixed aim and some tip between stops. Jump locks the aim and fires you after a short delay. **The catch:** a fuse burns down while you sit inside, and if it runs out the keg backfires, out of the rear and slower, and leaves you sooty. Held items stay in your pocket inside a keg. A shot that falls back into its own keg gets caught again.
  - **Y-Fling** (the slingshot, a keg mode): you sit in the pouch, visible, and pull the stick away from where you want to go; the pouch draws back and the aim is the other way, any of eight directions. The longer you pull, the harder it throws, from a weak flick to full power; let go of the stick and the pull eases off. Jump fires. **The catch:** sit in it too long and the band snaps, drops you, and takes a while to grow back. Held items work in the pouch.
- Props are assembled from **parts**: blocks of various materials, spikes, swinging heads, launchers, emitters, motion, springs, fans and bombs. A part's numbers are rolled once per match, and its name comes from them.
- **Each match has a short roster.** The seed picks the parts, and every crate that match draws only from them, so players learn a small set. A roster has 8 parts for 1–2 players and one more per player after that, up to 14 for 8 players: at least 4 more than a crate has cards, so crates differ round to round while every part keeps coming back. A player joining between rounds only adds parts.
  - Every roster holds the bridge part, a demolition part, a mystery crate, three hazards, a utility part and another building part. The rest are any other part, one of each kind.
- **Parody batch** (docs/PARODY-BACKLOG.md):
  - **Launch Plate** (the jump pad): a floor plate that *sets* your speed to its throw from its own center, so every launch flies the same arc whatever you came in with, and lands on a chalk X drawn where the real controller lands from it (if you don't steer; you still can). The place phase dots in the whole flight. It always lies on a floor: rotation picks the throw, right or mirrored left, flat and long or a higher, shorter lob.
  - **Four-Square** (the falling-block pieces): a shape a platform part can roll, one of the seven four-cell pieces in one colour each, the long one half as often. Plain footing that comes in one size, and never stands in for the bridge.
  - **Boost Strip** (the dash panel): an attachment like the coats. Touch it and you're held at about 1.6× run speed along its arrow for half a second, with no skid; its arrow points a quarter turn on from its face, so on a floor it runs right, under a ceiling left, up a wall's left face and down its right. On a wall it carries you along the face at that speed while you touch it. Weather doesn't stop it.
  - **Panic Button** (the P switch): a button and a dashed outline of 3–6 blocks. Press it (walk over it or bump it) and the blocks are solid for 5 s, blinking faster in the last second; pressing again restarts the time. A block never fills in around a runner: it waits until they're clear. The proof counts the blocks as gone.
  - **Fuse Crate** (the TNT crate): a crate you can stand on. Landing on it lights a 3-2-1 count; then it blasts everyone within about a tile and a half and is gone for a few seconds, coming back only when nobody's in its way. A shot, a flame, a laser, an arc or another crate's blast touching it sets it off at once, so crates chain. The blast clears no terrain and no props. It's a hazard: kills go to whoever placed it.
- **Variants differ in size only.** Within a match a part has one name, one look and one behavior; it comes small, medium or large, and a crate can show it at more than one size. Small and large cards say so in the name. Parts with nothing to scale (launchers, lasers, springs, launch plates, hangars, mystery crates, kegs, slingshots, fuse crates, Four-Squares) come in one size. Cards draw every size of a part at one scale.
- **A match can always be finished.** At every moment there's a plan of at most two bridges, each a spot the bridge card can go, with which the real controller reaches the flag over terrain and plain blocks while keeping clear of every other part (anything a part holds for most of its cycle: hazards, coats, springs, fans, slippery or moving blocks), with time bombs counted as gone off. A level starts from its recorded solution, which every set piece must leave standing. A placement (or instant bomb) after which no such plan exists is refused: the ghost turns red with a buzz and the player places elsewhere. Every crate contains at least one hazard and as many medium bridge cards as the plan still has bridges, never fewer than one. Past those cards no card repeats. After rounds where nobody finishes, crates quietly lean toward bombs, building parts and springs on the roster, gently after one dry round and more from the second, up to a cap. Crate contents are tied to the level seed and round, but the card order is shuffled with a per-match salt the host rolls, so it differs every game.
- **Secret Parts** (a rule) hides the parts. The crate deals role cards instead (Build, Hazard, Utility, Demolish), each an inked card in its role's color with its label and nothing else, in the same mix of roles the crate would have dealt, dry-round lean included. Each is still claimed by one player, first come first served. Claiming one rolls the part right then, from the match roster filtered to that role, weighted like the crate and seeded with the claimer and the frame: the same on every peer, and nobody can know it in advance. The part is revealed when place starts, to everyone: the claimer needs to see what they're placing, a shared couch screen can't hide one player's ghost from the rest, and seeing what the others drew is the payoff of the gamble. The crate guarantee holds: the role card standing in for each bridge card is a build card, and it rolls the bridge while the plan still needs one, so claiming every build card always yields the bridges. Mystery crates and held items are unchanged.
- **Placement is on the tile grid; a part's solid shape needn't be.** A part takes whole tiles to place, and those cells are its for placing and for the winnability proof. Inside them its solids can be any shape (a pipe's lip standing proud of its shaft), as long as the art matches them (docs/ART.md, fair play), you can stand on every top it shows, and no gap the shape leaves in its cells can catch or snag a runner. A sub-tile step a part means you to walk over climbs like a ledge lip. Rounded shapes are allowed the same way: the solids step to follow the curve to within about a pixel, every step low enough to walk or jump round, so a round rim is as round to stand on as it looks.
- **A part takes the tiles its object does.** Most are one tile, but nothing has to be: a launcher whose object is long or tall takes two (`LAUNCHER_FRAMES`). The hangar is its hangar and the runway its planes roll out along, and the pirate cannon its carriage (2×1); the ballista stands on its tower, the vending machine, the tiki post and the gumball machine stand tall (1×2). Machines whose object is a squat block (arrow trap, turret, mortar, sentry, jets, emitters) stay one tile. A bigger part is harder to fit and blocks more, which is the trade for reading as what it is: a tall one is a wall you wall-jump, a long one a ledge you climb. Its shots leave from where its art shows the muzzle.
  - **Machines stand upright.** A quarter turn turns a launcher onto a wall, but a half turn mirrors it: it faces the other way on the same footing, its sub-tile steps mirrored, and a lobber still lobs (`Xform.U`).
- **Every part can always be placed somewhere**, or a pick could stall the place phase on a level without the right surface.
- **Attachments** (spike strips, ice coats, slime coats, snap traps) must mount flush on an exposed face of a solid block, and rotation picks the face.
  - A strip too long for its spot still places: only the cells that can mount land, the rest are gone for good, and the ghost shows exactly what lands.
  - Ice makes floors slippery and walls slick (no wall jump).
  - Slime makes floors slow and walls sticky (you cling).
- **Conveyors have one belt face, and rotation picks it**: top, right, bottom or left, with the belt running the same way round the block, so a belt carrying you right on top runs down the right face, left along the bottom and up the left face. Every other face is a plain block.
  - A top belt carries walkers.
  - A side belt grips a runner clinging to it (holding into it in the air) and carries them at its own speed: running up, it's an escalator that lifts you over its top edge; running down, it lowers you. It never slows a jump that's already rising faster than it. Wall jumps work as usual, and a belt running up adds its speed to the jump; one running down takes nothing away.
  - A bottom belt flings a head that bonks it sideways along it at twice its speed, about a run. That keeps the underside a consequence you see coming, without a new verb like clinging under it.
  - The winnability plan keeps clear of conveyors at every rotation, like any part that isn't plain footing.
- Bombs clear terrain and/or props depending on their kind. Attachments fall off when their host block is destroyed.
- **Parts ride what they're stuck to.** A block (any material, a spike block, a mystery box) placed flush against a moving solid, or a coat or spike strip mounted on a moving face, becomes that mover's rider and follows it for the rest of the match. A coat on a lift is a slime lift; a block on a wheel's car is a step that goes round with it.
  - **Translation only.** Physics is axis-aligned, so a rider follows its mover's travel and never turns: a coat on a wheel's car rides round with the car and keeps its face, as the car itself stays level.
  - **What carries:** the blocks of a part that moves (a moving platform, a lift, a track car), a wheel's or pendulum's cars, and a cart's body, set pieces included (a slime coat on the Great Wheel's cars, spikes under a lift). Not a crumbling block (it breaks and would leave its riders floating), a blink block (half the time it isn't there), a crusher (when it moves depends on who walks under it, so the proof can't follow it), a swinging blade or a rotating saw (no solid to stick to), runners, or gliders.
  - **What rides:** blocks, mystery boxes and coats, things that hold still and move as one piece. Parts that aim, cast or hold a runner from a fixed mount (launchers, springs, fans, ropes, pipes, snap traps) and anything with motion of its own place by the usual rules.
  - **Chains:** a coat on a block that rides a lift rides the lift too.
  - **Placing.** Movers keep moving while you place, so a part sticks to where its mover is on the frame it goes in: within three quarters of a tile it snaps flush against the moving face. The ghost rides along, shows the loop it will travel as a dotted line with a bead running round it, and wears a chain link where it touches what carries it. Touching a mover means riding it: a part whose ride would take it into rock, off the level's side or into a protected zone anywhere along the way is refused, never placed still beside the path. A coat goes to whichever wins more of its cells, the still faces or one mover, and the still faces win a tie.
  - **Bombs.** A rider goes with what it rides: blast the mover and everything stuck to it goes too, chains included; blast a rider and only it and what rides it go. Nothing falls and nothing is left floating. A blast hits a rider where it is when the blast goes off.
  - The winnability proof counts a rider wherever its mover takes it, like any mover; it's never footing the proof stands on.
- **Movers and traps with a catch.** Warped pipes (below), bumpers (aimable kick), gravity wells / repulsors (bend jumps and drag you along the ground), webs (catch falls, slow you), blink blocks (solid on a timer, pairs alternate, never reappear inside you), cog wheels (ride platforms), dropstones (slam when you pass under, ride them back up) and snap traps (face-mounted spikes that pop when you linger). Every timed or triggered part shows a tell first.
- **Warped pipes** (the plumber's warp pipe) only work in pairs.
  - Each pipe is its own card and its own placement, taking a footprint two tiles across and 2, 3 or 4 tiles long (its small, medium and large). Its solid is a shaft a tile and a half across with a lip the full two tiles across and half a tile deep at the mouth end, so the lip stands out a quarter tile on each side. The lip is a rolled rim, round at every corner, and the shaft's far corners are softened; the solids step to follow both. Rotation picks the way the mouth faces. You stand on the lip and on the shaft's far end like a block, and walk over a sideways lip's quarter-tile step without jumping.
  - Pipes link in the order they go in over the match: the 1st with the 2nd, the 3rd with the 4th. An odd one waits for a partner, its mouth a dead black hole, and is only a block. A pair stays a pair while both stand. When a bomb takes one, its partner waits again and pairs with the next pipe placed, or at once with a pipe already waiting, oldest first. So there's never more than one pipe waiting. Links are sim state, set as parts go in and come out.
  - Going in: on a linked mouth facing up, stand over the opening and hold down. Under one facing down, hold up (or jump) with your head against it. At a sideways mouth, push into it, on foot or in the air. You slide in (a third of a second), come out of the partner's mouth facing its way (another third), and leave it with a hop, a walk or a drop, whichever way it faces. If the far mouth is blocked when you get there, the pipe spits you back out of the near one. Whatever would send you straight back into the mouth you came out of (down, out of one facing up) has to be let go and pressed again first.
  - A trip is a hold, like a keg: your hitbox waits just outside the mouth you're at, so a hazard there still gets you, other runners pass through you, and your held item stays in your pocket. One runner per pipe at a time. Only runners travel: shots and loose items never go through.
  - One pipe part per match, so every pipe that match is one material, rolled with the part: classic green, brass, rusty iron, porcelain or verdigris copper. A crate can show more than one pipe card, at different sizes. While a pipe waits for its partner, crates (and Secret Parts rolls) weight the pipe 4 times as heavily.
  - Pipes are utility parts. The winnability plan keeps clear of a pipe and its mouth like any part that isn't plain footing, so a match never relies on one, and the crate guarantee never counts one as a bridge.
  - Linked pairs wear a matching numbered job tag, in place and in the race, and a linked mouth breathes a faint draft. Both ends flare as someone goes through. While you place a pipe, a dotted line ties it to the pipe it would pair with.
- **Blood is slippery.** A runner who dies on or near a surface oozes a short puddle of their own body color along the floor under them (or a smear down a wall with no floor near), and it's ice underfoot for everyone, them included. Walls keep their wall jump. By default it's gone at the end of the next round (spilled in round R, it clears when R+1 ends), and the Blood Rounds rule sets that to 1, 2 or 3 rounds, or off for none at all. A new death on the same spot renews it. Pits leave nothing, protected zones stay clean, and it only lands on terrain and plain still blocks, so coats, conveyors and the like keep working. A blast clears every cell it touches, and blood goes with its block or tile, or under a block or coat placed over it.
- **Status effects are plain, short and visible**: frozen (no control, ~0.8 s, ice block) and slowed (capped speed and jumps, ~2 s, goo drips). The spawn zone shields you from them, and from fields and kicks, as it does from hazards.
- **Launcher aim is legible.** Aim rolls fixed 7 : sweep 2 : track 1.5, so most launchers hold a fixed direction. Trackers only watch ±60° of their mount, always carry a tracking word in their name, show a red sensor camera, and crate cards and the codex state the aim (Fixed, Sweeps, Tracks).
- **Sentry guns** (the team shooter's engineer sentry, our own tripod gun): a squat turret that sweeps its cone until a runner is in range, inside that cone and in its sight. Then it beeps, its lamp turns red, and it turns after them no faster than its turn rate, so a runner close in can outrun it. After a short spin-up behind a dotted laser sight, it fires one burst of fast rounds along its barrel, then cools down with its lamp amber. **Its rounds kill**, like every launcher's (items are what bonk), and stop at terrain and solids like every shot. Breaking its sight before the spin-up ends stops the lock. Between bursts it always holds fire longer than a route needs to slip past (the same opening timed hazards give), so it never seals a level, and the plan treats it like any launcher: only its block is in the way. It comes small, medium and large, and only its range differs.
- **Projectiles vary.** Only one kind homes: the seeker, which is rare, slower than a run, has a blinking red seeker lens and is tagged HOMING on its card. The rest: boomerangs, rubber balls (bounce; shove, don't kill), cluster shells (split at the top of the arc), goo and frost (non-lethal; slow or freeze you, coat open terrain in slime or ice for a few seconds, never inside a protected zone or a one-tile gap), ricochets (bank off walls) and **gliders** (a launcher fires one-way planks you can ride: they fly level, then sink, faster the more riders they carry, and can never pin you).
- **Demolition kinds**: bomb (disk, everything), EMP (props only), drill (terrain-only bore in the rotation direction, leaving props alone), cross and ring charges, and time bombs (go off as the race starts, so they catch whatever was placed that round; a charge caught in an earlier blast is defused). The placement ghost shows every affected cell and outlines the props that will go.
- **Solids stop whatever travels.** Flames, lasers, geysers, fan columns, shots, crushers and tracker sight stop at terrain and at any block that's solid on that frame, so a block is a shield; a tesla arc needs a clear line between its coils. One-way platforms, gliders and a part's own body don't stop them, and runners never do: a beam goes through everyone in it.
- **Updrafts are steady**: inside a fan column you rise or are pushed at a constant speed, standing in a column never stops you from jumping, and the visuals match exactly when it pushes.

## Rules ("level settings", set by the host)
- Match Length: default Normal. See Scoring.
- Pick timer: default 15 s. When it runs out, stragglers get a random leftover prop.
- Place timer: default 15 s. When it runs out, an unplaced prop is forfeited.
- Race timer: default off.
- Gravity: default Auto.
- Weather: default on. Off makes every level clear.
- Blood rounds: how many rounds a puddle lasts, default 2. Off means no blood at all.
- Secret Parts: default off. See Props.
- A rule the level is built from (gravity, weather) rebuilds the lobby's level on the spot, for everyone.
- The Rules card is one screen with no scrolling at 720p: a label and a row of choices per rule, no explanations.

## Controls and UI
- A picked part's card leaves the crate entirely. The other cards stay where they are.
- On PC, keyboard and mouse both work. One keyboard player can use WASD *and* the arrows. A "Split keyboard" setting turns the keyboard into two local players. Everything can be remapped.
- The mouse drives the menu cursor: click to pick or place, right-click or the wheel to rotate. They do nothing else, so a stray click can't self-destruct.
- **Self-destruct is a deliberate hold on every input**: alt (R or Q, B or LB, the 💥 button on touch) held for 3 s (`POP_HOLD`). A ring fills around your runner for everyone to see, and on your own screen a big 3, 2, 1 counts down in the middle; couch players charging at once share it side by side, each in their color. Remote players' charges show only the ring. It starts only on a fresh press during the race, and letting go, steering, jumping or using an item ends it; the next try needs a new press. So a hold carried over from place (where alt rotates) or a thumb resting on a button never counts. The sim counts the hold from the held alt in each frame's input, so every peer pops you on the same frame.
- A gamepad's cursor ramps up while the stick is held: a tap moves it a little, a long push crosses the screen fast.
- **Touch (mobile)**: left half is a floating stick (push past ~70% to run), right half is jump (hold for height), self-destruct is a small 💥 button under Settings, and a Use button appears above jump while you hold an item. Pick by tapping a card; place by dragging a ghost that floats above your finger, with ROTATE and PLACE buttons. Landscape first.
- **Phones see the game closer up**, and it stays render-only: a phone's camera is its own, and cursors stay in world coordinates so every peer sees them land in the same place. Solo, the race camera follows you with a tile at least ~24 CSS px, with look-ahead and a vertical dead zone, clamped to the level. **With more than one player in the match, local or remote, every live runner stays on screen, and that always beats the tile size.** Pick and place start zoomed out, with pinch-zoom, two-finger pan, and auto-pan when a ghost nears an edge. HUD text, buttons and crate cards scale up, and every tap target is at least 44 CSS px.
- In the lobby, a stray click does nothing; only buttons act.
- UI copy is proper title case, with no twee lowercase taglines.
- Inputs are shown as **button prompts** (keycap, gamepad and mouse glyphs) in a footer, the way console games do it, and are never spelled out in prose.
- Settings are tabs, one screen each with no scrolling at 720p (on phones, a full-screen sheet with big rows that scrolls): Audio, Video, Keyboard, Mouse, Gamepad (Touch replaces the last three under touch). The same menu opens on the home screen and in-game, and works with mouse, touch, keyboard (Q/E tabs, arrows) and gamepad (LB/RB tabs, Start opens, B closes). It reopens on the last tab of that page load. Reset Controls shows on the Keyboard tab only, since nothing else it resets is on screen.
- Empty lobby slots show **OPEN**.

## Online
- The URL `/r/<code>` identifies a lobby; the homepage is a normal main menu. There's no matchmaking, and you join only through a shared link (or by typing its code under Join a Lobby).
- **Arriving.** Opening a room shows its code while the link comes up, with a token for each peer already there, then drops you in. If it can't (Online Not Set Up, Room Is Full, Cannot Reach Host, Different Version, Kicked) it says which, with Play Offline (except when kicked) and Main Menu. An empty room's link just starts that room: there's no "not found".
- **Seats.** Arriving in the lobby seats you without pressing anything, up to 8 players. Arriving while a match runs, or in a full lobby, you **watch**: the live match, with the race camera framing everyone and every cursor and ghost in pick and place, and nothing to control but the menus. On a phone that's no touch controls, just the view. A `?watch` link arrives watching wherever the match is.
- **Join** is a primary button in the bottom corner for anyone watching, in every phase, with the confirm key or button on it. With no seat free it reads **Match Full** and does nothing, and it lights up the moment one frees. It opens the **join card** over the live match: your character big on a lobby card, the New Look dice (rerolls only: there's no picker), your name to edit, the seats as they stand, and Join. Cancel goes back to watching. In the lobby and between rounds the seat is yours at once; mid-round the card says **You're In Next Round** and you're seated at the round boundary.
- **The host decides a race for the last seat.** A Join is a request the host puts in order with everything else, so when two ask for one seat the first it hears wins, on every peer alike. The other's card says **Taken**, they go back to watching, and Join reads Match Full. A seat won mid-round counts as taken for anyone asking after. A second click is the same request; a request in flight when the host goes is decided by the new host, once; a winner who leaves before the boundary gives the seat back.
- **Gone players.** A player whose peer drops mid-match (a closed tab, a dead phone, a lost connection) is **away** at once on every screen: an unplugged icon on their race chip, score row and lobby card (AFK is a tag; gone is the icon), and their runner pops. Back within **40 s**, they carry on in their seat. Gone longer, they're dropped: their seat is free from then (Join lights up for watchers) and they leave at the round boundary. Their parts stay in the level. If they come back later they watch, and a Join seats them with their look and points as they were.
- **Rooms end.** A room nobody is in, players or watchers, for **60 s** is over: its link starts a fresh room under the same code, on the code's own level. Before then, the first one back picks the match up where it was. "Active" means this, here and in any list of rooms.
- **The round boundary** is the score screen: seats won mid-round are taken there, in the order they were won, and a seat freed mid-round (below) frees there.
- **Watch** is on your own lobby card (the eye beside the dice) and in the Settings menu mid-match, never a stray key. In the lobby it frees your seat at once. Mid-match you play the round out, the corner says **Watching Next Round**, and your runner leaves as the round is scored. Choosing Join again before then keeps the seat.
- **Watchers in the lobby** are a row of small chips in the online panel: an eye and a name each (AFK tagged), yours outlined. The host kicks a watcher from its chip, like a player from their card, in the lobby only.
- The host wears a `HOST` label in the lobby, and it moves with the host; a remote player's card shows their ping in the same spot, and there's no separate list of names. The host can kick a player from the lobby only, by mouse, or with Use, then Confirm on keyboard or gamepad. **A kick keeps that browser out for 60 s**, however it comes back: a reload, a new tab, the invite link. The kicked player sees Kicked with "You can rejoin in 0:59" counting down and Main Menu; at zero, Rejoin. Trying the link again inside the minute shows the same screen. The host can't lift a ban early.
- **Host = the earliest-joined connected player.** When the host leaves, the next player in join order takes over seamlessly, never a watcher while a player is here: a watcher steps to the back of the line. A host who starts watching hands the room to the next player. When only watchers are left, the earliest of them hosts until someone takes a seat. When everyone leaves, the next arrival becomes host. A refresh is a new join: mid-match you get your slot, look and score back, in the lobby your look, a watcher stays a watcher, and a host who refreshes comes back as a guest.
- Netcode sends inputs, not state, over a deterministic simulation that must be bit-identical across browsers. Snapshots are only for joining, resync and host migration. The host is the decider: when a guest's state drifts, the host's state wins.
- Peers connect over WebRTC in a star around the host. A guest that can't reach the host directly (and has no TURN server) drops to local play. Details: `docs/NETCODE.md`.
- In pick and place everyone sees everyone's cursor and held part live. A card belongs to whoever the host hears first; nobody sees a claim that gets taken back.

## Platform
- Built with Next.js on Vercel. All game logic runs client-side. The server renders thin route shells and runs small, stateless signaling routes for online play.
- **Every screen is part of the game, so it's portable.** Main menu, lobby, settings, codex, 404 and error screens are the game's own, in `src/`, with one look, one logo and one input system. Next is just one host. Screens are reached from the game's menus, so a build with no address bar reaches all of them; `/`, `/r/<code>` and `/codex` are deep links too. Moving between screens never reloads the page, except leaving the error screen: Play opens a new lobby, Join checks the code first, and leaving a lobby (Leave Lobby, or Main Menu from the Kicked screen) goes back to the main menu. Debug params such as `?seed` and `?theme` stay on the URL throughout.
