# Game design decisions

This is a concise log of decided rules. It's the source of truth for gameplay intent. When a decision changes, update this file in the same commit. Implementation details live in AGENTS.md and the code. The full log of the owner's decisions, with dates, status and what each one replaced, is [DECISIONS.md](DECISIONS.md).

## Lore
Design guidance, never game text (see Discovery). When a choice is unclear, ask what fits this world.

> **Slag** is what's left over. Every game anyone loves is poured from the Great Foundry, and the runoff cools into Slag: a scrap world of melted-down levels (a farm here, a castle there, half a space station) and the characters who never made the cut: the knock-off heroes who almost got their own game.
>
> Once a night the Foundry opens a single mould. Whoever reaches the flag gets cast into the next real game. Everyone builds the course together from whatever scrap tumbles out of the crate, and everyone sabotages it, because there's only one mould.

What it explains: themes are melted-down levels (game-like places, slightly off); the cast is rejects and knock-offs, so parody belongs; parts are scrap from other games; one mould means one winner, and a course everyone finishes earns nobody the mould.

## Core loop
- Lobby → **Pick** (claim one prop from a crate) → **Place** (put it in the level) → **Race** to the flag → **Score**. Repeat until someone reaches the target score or the round limit, whichever comes first, then **Victory**.
- A placement can be raced more than once before the next pick: each race on it is a **bout** (Bouts per Pick, a lobby rule; Brawl plays three, the other modes one). Every bout is a round of its own: it scores, victory is checked after it, and it counts toward the round limit.
- Matches have 1–8 players. Players can be local (any mix of keyboard, gamepads and touch) or online.
- Props persist for the whole match, so levels get more dangerous every round.
- Round 1 is often unfinishable, especially with few players. That's expected: nearly every level needs parts, and parts build up over the match.

## Discovery
- Part of the joy of the game is the unknown. Players learn parts, rules and tricks by playing and by watching each other.
- The game never explains the obvious: no sub lines, hints or notes that restate what's on screen or spell out a rule. If the screen can show it, don't say it. The main menu's short sub lines are the one exception, kept as part of the home screen's look.
- **The codex, subtitled "Spoilers!", is where you go to be spoiled.** Its parts are the global catalog: every part, item and projectile drawn live, with a name, a one-line joke and the numbers, and items still unexplained. Its cast is every kind of runner, drawn live by its sound-alike name with a one-line joke, running through its moves up close; a kind with no codex line fails `tsc`. Its maps are every theme in turn: the place as a plain level of it, then each of its landmarks with its name filled with a sample place, what it is and its ways through, then each hazard only that theme has and what it does. Every map picture is the game's own renderer on a real level from a fixed seed, a landmark framed on its box, a hazard at the busiest frame of its cycle; up close the race plays. The maps read the registries, so a new theme, landmark or unique hazard is in them without a codex edit. Describing mechanics is fine there; restating what the picture shows still isn't. Every card has a permalink, `/codex/<section>/<card>`, built from registry keys rather than its parody name, which may change; opening a card puts it in the URL, back and forward open and close cards, and a link the codex doesn't know is the plain codex.

## Parody
- Our goal is to parody other games, especially with items. Players should recognize what's being mocked and laugh at the twist.
- What we parody: the build-then-race party platformer is the core loop's inspiration. Nintendo's platformers are the main well: homages across many franchises, the plumber's blocks, pipes and shells, the ape's barrels and girders, and more. The big crossover brawler is the model for items and stage hazards: lots of them, chaotic and readable. Past those, any iconic pop-culture game is fair game, as the team shooter's sentry gun was; [PARODY-BACKLOG.md](PARODY-BACKLOG.md) ranks the candidates.
- A parody is ours: our own name, our own art and a comic twist on the idea. Never the original's real name, art, characters or level designs.
- **One credit by name.** The About screen credits the core concept to Ultimate Chicken Horse (Clever Endeavour Games), outright. It names nothing else it parodies.
- Mock the trope before any one game; a nod at one game gets a sound-alike or spoof name. Each part's target, silhouette and visual gag are in [PARODY.md](PARODY.md).
- Props are objects, not characters. No item, part, projectile or set piece has a face or a persona (eyes, a mouth, an expression, little feet, a cap worn like a character); only runners have faces. The parody comes through the object's shape, material and mechanism.
- **Player characters may parody famous characters**: a mustached plumber, a jellyfish-brained floater, a hard-hat engineer, a goose up to no good. Each is our own design under a sound-alike name, recognisable at a glance as the homage, never the real character, its name or its art. Parts and machines stay faceless.

## Scoring
- Finishing earns +10 points. The first player to finish gets an extra +4.
- If you finish, each rival killed by your prop, or by the level right after your bonk, earns you +2. Traps alone don't pay: you have to reach the flag too.
- A kill is credited to whoever last knocked the victim (an item hit, a bump, a body check) if it comes within 2.5 s of the knock, whatever the knock threw them into, a part of theirs or someone else's included; otherwise to whoever placed what killed them. A fall of your own, or into your own part, pays nobody.
- If you finish while trailing the leader, after 2 or more rounds in a row without a point, you earn an underdog bonus of +8, most of a finish again, as the reference game pays its comeback (docs/MODES.md → Race). In Rising Slag and Brawl a trailing scorer's bonus stays +3.
- If everyone finishes, nobody scores ("too easy"). If nobody finishes, nobody scores either.
- Solo play just rewards finishing.
- A match always ends: scoreless rounds can run forever, so the round limit has no "off" setting.
- The lobby sets both win conditions with one **Match Length**: Short (30 points or 10 rounds), Normal (50 points or 15 rounds, the default) or Long (80 points or 25 rounds). The limit is meant to decide some matches, mostly with 2 players, where a round only scores if exactly one player finishes.
- At the end of a match the top score wins and ties are joint winners. If the round limit hits with nobody on the board, there is no winner.
- Anything that lists scores shows the most points first, with ties in slot order. This is display only: player order in the sim never changes. On the score screen every row is a lane racing to one finish line at the target score: this round's awards drop in as blocks, one reason at a time, and the rows slide to their new rank as they land.

## Modes
- **Every mode has a reference game**, and takes its mechanics and its targets from how that game plays: Race from the build-then-race party platformer, Rising Slag from the lead-camera racer, Brawl from the one-hit party brawler, Gauntlet from the co-op puzzle platformer. [MODES.md](MODES.md) lists each mode's attributes and measurements beside its reference's, with sources.
- **A mode is another way to play the same match**: the same pick and place, parts, items, characters and art, played for something else. Each mode is an entry in `MODES` (`src/game/modes.ts`): the level it asks worldgen for, what a race is played for (`RunRules`: a flag or not, last one standing or not), how its crates are dealt (`CrateMix`) and how a round scores. A level is built for its mode, so changing the mode in the lobby rebuilds the level, for everyone.
- **The mode is the lobby's first choice.** It leads the lobby's top bar as the **Mode plate** (the mode's emblem and its name; on a phone, its emblem by Rules), and opens the **Mode card**: one big tile per mode, its emblem and its name, nothing else. Picking one rebuilds the level and closes the card. The host's mode is kept with their preferences, so Play opens the last one. The lobby list says a room's mode when it isn't a race.
- **Every level set builds every mode's level**: the engine builds the level, so a mode is a level request and a set its look and shapes. A set that can't yet still plays, and the Level card marks it (`FARM · RISING SLAG WIP`, `ModeDef.builds`); today every set builds both modes.
- **Race** is the game as it was built: run for the flag.
- **Brawl**: knock each other into the level, the last one standing taking the round. The arena is a classic one-screen level of its own, built from its set's pieces for knocking people off things: a platform fighter's stage, as the crossover brawler's are (owner decision): a main stage floating over the drop with air between both its ends and the sides of the map, laid in thick slabs a few columns wide with seams of drop between them, and boards you jump up through in layers straight over the slabs, so nearly every place you stand is a knock from a fall that kills. Nothing stands under the stage but the drop; the slabs' ends are wall faces a knocked runner slides down and jumps back up from. Its two ends mirror each other so neither starts with the high ground. A hall, a canopy, a court (a slab wider than the rest) and the sky over the top boards are room for whatever its set stands on a floor, hangs from a ceiling or mounts on a wall. Its outline varies (flat, stepped up at the ends, peaked or dipped in the middle), it raises no landmark (the arena is the stage), and it owes no part, each end reaching the other. It's deadly from the first round: six set pieces, and only ones that kill or knock (no platform or lift), from the nastiest of its mix, nearly all six in every set's arenas. It takes two to start, bots counting; alone, the lobby waits, its count reading `1/2 Players`. Half the field starts at each end. It never mists: mist would hide the edges that kill. Nothing kills on its own: items knock, bumps knock and bodies shove, on the race's own physics with bodies shoving harder (twice a race's shove, and a run-up into someone who isn't pushing back is a body check that knocks them like a bump: Players → Side contact), and what kills is the level: a hazard you're knocked or pushed into, the drop, a part someone placed. Hits build up, as a platform fighter's damage does: every rival's hit you take in a bout (an item, a bump from below, a body check) throws the next one farther, a quarter as hard again per hit up to six, so a fresh runner holds a slab and someone hit four times (twice as hard, a knock carrying about four times as far) flies at a tap. Only rivals' hits count, never the level's own pushes; it never wears off, so hiding doesn't heal you, and every bout starts everyone at none. You see it, never a number: whoever's been hit smoulders, a pale wisp off the head at one or two hits, a grey plume with sparks spitting off the body and hot feet at three or four, and from five a black plume, more sparks and the feet glowing. A placement is fought over three times (three bouts, Bouts per Pick) before the next pick, and parts stay, so the arena gets nastier through a match: with one fight a placement, picking and placing took 28% of a match of four Normal bots (about 9 s each time, against 20 s a fight), and with three, 12%. A row of pips under the clock says which bout it is. Everyone starts each bout holding something, a starter good for one go, so the fight starts at GO (owner decision: Brawl leans on items). After that, weapons come from the arena's own stands, as the party brawler's spawners hand them out: three or four, mirrored, on the floor near the tier the field starts on and in the middle, each showing what it holds bobbing over a game-show podium. Walk into one empty-handed and it's yours; 5 s later another stands there, its bulbs lighting one by one while its silhouette fills with gold. None stands in or beside a set piece, in a hazard's reach or in a gun's line, and they come in mirrored pairs, an odd one on the middle. Full hands walk past (toss yours to swap). Starters and stands deal only things that knock someone into something, the swings most: the noodle and the bat twice as often and the Sucker Punch three times, the Rude Awakening and the Catch of the Day twice each, for getting at someone a swing can't reach (on a ledge across a shaft, over a lip), then the shell, the boomerang, the brick and the Upper Hand. Nothing that only moves you, and no Bubble Wrap, which would soak the very hit the mode kills with. The slag is the last resort: until late in a round the drop is only a drop, and 25 s in slag starts pouring up from out of sight under the arena (in view about 4 s later), slowly and then faster, so a stalemate always ends; knocks do the killing. A round runs 60 s at most. A brawl pays knockouts, not survival (owner decision: you shouldn't be able to win by just standing around): +5 for every rival you knock out (a kill credited to you: Scoring), standing or not, and +5, one knockout's worth, for the last one standing, so knocking out two and then falling beats hiding to the end. A draw (everyone left out at once, or the time out) has no winner but keeps its knockouts. A trailing scorer's underdog bonus (+3) keeps the comeback. The camera frames the whole pack. Its crates lean to hazards and utility parts. Bots press the fight: they close on the nearest rival and would rather line them up with whatever would kill them, but take the hit they have after about a second in reach (a moment's aim when none would throw them anywhere), charge a rival a body check would throw into something, dodge only a hit that's coming, fetch from the nearest stand when their hands are empty (one holding something that knocks counting nearer), and climb from the slag only once it's close; how pushy is a trait, Hard bots most. They place their hazards where a knock throws someone, never by the ends. Rounds aim at 15–30 s (owner decision), and how often items are used is counted a minute of fighting, not a round: a short round isn't fewer swings. Four Normal bots draw 4% of the rounds on the bare arena and 2% with parts placed, which run 17.7 s and 17.8 s, using 47 and 41 items a minute; 77% and 78% of deaths come off a rival's knock, and the slag takes 6 of 146 and 10 of 193 (`BRAWL_ROUNDS`).
- **Rising Slag**: the Foundry pours. You climb a **looping shaft**, and slag rises after you. A shaft is one lap of its theme's own storeys, 48 rows, with terrain walls up both sides, 48 to 56 columns wide so its walls sit on the screen's edges. Climb out of its top and you carry on from the bottom, a lap on: it's drawn as one shaft scrolling up forever, with no seam to see. Its top storey is its spawn storey over again, and a lap is proven climbable bare: no part is ever needed, and parts are what players put in each other's way. Every part placed is met once a lap, and parts stay for the match, so the loop gets nastier every round. The spawn storey is the loop's seam and is protected like the spawn: nothing is placed there. Every set builds one: the shaft's structure is the tall builder's, and each set brings its own floors, connectors and dressing (a castle's stone floors and stair flights, a farm's barn lofts with earth floors and plank loft boards, a harbor's pier decks and crate stacks, a space station's hull decks and ladders), its look doing the rest. There is no flag.
  - **Fall off the bottom of the leader's screen and you're out** (owner decision, 2026-11-07), as in the lead-camera racer. The screen is the arena: a window 29 rows tall rides over whoever has climbed highest, laps and all, easing after them so a jump doesn't bob it, with the leader 8 rows under its top. The leader runs free (owner decision): the window follows them however fast they climb, never letting their head out of its top or their feet within 4 rows of its bottom. Whoever is wholly under its bottom edge, 21 rows under the leader, for three quarters of a second is out; climb back in sooner and it's forgiven. The camera shows exactly that window, its bottom edge on the bottom of the screen and its top under the name tags, so what you see is what kills. Whatever shows over its top (the tags' row, more on a window of another shape) is drawn shut behind the tags, like the footer's bar, with a plain edge. The bottom edge glows hot under anyone near it, and whoever drops under it gets a chevron in their color on a soot disc just over the footer, its ring running out with their grace. With one runner there's no screen (the camera frames them and the slag, as below). The field starts on a **start line** across the spawn storey's floor, four cells apart, all of it in the screen at GO.
  - **Bots see what the screen shows** (owner decision): the window and a row or two over its top, no further. Every tile beyond is unknown to them, and so is whatever stands there (boxes, items, rivals, parts). Until the lap line comes into view a bot climbs to the highest place it can see and get to, under a column of its own, and looks again as rows come into view, as a player climbs toward the top edge of the screen. A leader bot finds a way round a wall over the screen only once it's in sight.
  - **The slag is the floor that ends a stalled pack.** It starts a storey under the spawn's floor, boils for 3 s, then rises at least at its floor pace (1.2 tiles/s, gaining 0.05 a second), slow enough that a slow lobby climbs ahead of it and a leader can't hold it back by dawdling. And it's on a leash to whoever has climbed highest, laps and all: it never falls more than the leash below the leader's feet, rushing up at 10 tiles/s when it has. On the first lap the leash is longer than the screen, so the screen's edge gets you first; from the second it's shorter, and the slag rises into the screen. Alone, the camera frames from a few rows over you down to the slag, and during the opening, while the leash is longer, that much further down, so the slag is in sight from GO.
  - **Laps follow the difficulty curve** (see Difficulty), the leash set as a racer's camera is, in time behind the leader: about 4.5 s of climbing on the first lap (25 rows), 3.5 s (19) on the second and 2.5 s (14) from the third on, a Normal climber's pace (5.5 rows a second) turning seconds into rows. At GO it's 16 rows longer, fading over 25 s, while the field untangles. From the first runner out, or 30 s after GO if nobody is, it shortens by 0.3 rows a second, down to 2, so even the leader goes in the end. The shaft is **open air**, not storeys: ledges staggered like bricks up the walls, each one starting where the gap below it does, so every height has several lines up and a pack can pass. Some are the theme's own floor (solid: go round), the rest boards you jump up through; the spawn storey's floor is boards outside its pocket, so the climb comes up through it. A **wall-jump chimney** runs up one side for half the lap, the quick way for whoever can climb a wall. The shaft is built to its level's difficulty: wide ledges, short gaps and mostly boards on gentle, rising three rows a step; narrower ledges and wider gaps on medium, with some ledges left out (**wells**, so a slip falls further); narrow ledges, near-full gaps, four-row steps and more wells on hard.
  - **The field scores by how long it outlasted the slag**: the last one standing +10, the last to fall before them +6, the one before that +3, so a round pays more than its winner, as a race pays every finisher. The first one out never scores, so a duel pays only its winner. Runners caught on the same frame share the better place, unless that's everyone: then nobody scores. Every kill pays its killer +2, standing or not, and a trailing scorer can earn the underdog bonus. The race ends the moment one runner is left.
  - **Every loop has mystery boxes of its own**, as a racer's tracks have item boxes: two to four up the shaft, about one every two storeys, each hanging over a floor where a climber knocks it from below, never on the seam's band or in a perch well, refilling every 5 s. They deal by rank, and the further behind you are the stronger, as a kart racer's do: a few rows off the lead you draw something mild that reaches up (the Upper Hand, the Social Climber twice, the Trickle-Down, a Pocket Block, now and then the Queue Jumper); 6 rows back the Queue Jumper twice, the Upper Hand, the Social Climber three times and the Trickle-Down; 12 rows back, half a screen, the Queue Jumper three times in five and the Social Climber; and the leader something to hold on with (Bubble Wrap, the Slippery Slope over the ledges under them, the Pocket Block). The winged shell and the noodle are out of a climb's deal: in a shaft they knocked 0.09 and 0.04 rivals a round. A climber 12 rows or more behind the leader, still on the leader's screen, with empty hands for a second doesn't need a box: a **Hand-Me-Down**, a parcel tied with string in their colour, drops in from the top of the screen onto them and hands them what a box deals that far back, again 8 s after it lands if they're still that far back with nothing in hand (owner decision, 2026-11-07: "Add items, no boxes"). A loop's box puts what it deals **straight into empty hands**, as a racer's item box does (a climb has no time to hop about after a pop, and its boxes hang in open air); with full hands it pops it out of the top like any box. Boxes players place keep their own random pool, which never holds the climb's own items.
  - **Every loop has Leg Days on its hard lines**: a protein shaker floating on the lip of the longest drops of each lap, two or more a lap at least twelve rows apart, nearly always the ledge ends over the wall-jump chimney or a well, where a slip falls past a row or many. Touch it and every jump, off the ground or a wall, takes off 1.3 times as fast for 4 s (a full jump 6.2 rows from 3.7: the next row and the one past it through a board are one jump); it's back 3 s after it's taken. Anyone can take it, the leader too, whatever they hold.
  - **Its crates** owe no bridge (a lap climbs bare), and lean to hazards and utility parts, away from building parts (a shortcut for whoever's ahead) and demolition. The leader-hunting winged shell goes after whoever has climbed highest, laps and all, since there's no flag to be near.
  - **Alone**, nobody can outlast you, so you score for how far up the slag had to come to catch you, out of 10.
  - Parts still go in every round, so a climb gets harder, or shorter, as the match goes on.
  - Bots climb as high as they can; they never self-destruct into the slag. Behind, they knock a box a short way off their climb when they could reach what it pops; leading, they don't leave the climb for one, since the slag is on their heels. They read the bottom of the leader's screen as they read the slag: near it, they leave boxes be and use what lifts them, and a knock that would drop a rival under it counts as one that kills.

## Players
- Characters are generated per player (kind, colors, face and name) and are **cosmetic only**: every player has the same hitbox and the same physics.
- **The cast is all homages**: thirty famous game characters to parody, in our own designs, each with a clear twist of its own (a mustached plumber, the blue speedster, a green-tunic elf, a yellow electric mouse, an impostor crewmate, a goose up to no good…; the list is CHARACTERS.md → The cast). **Recognisability comes first**: each keeps the three or four traits people know it by, so a stranger names it at a glance. **The player's color** is the costume, and every other coloured part is a tone of it; the rest of a kind's palette is neutral, so the shape carries the read. It matches the HUD. Each kind is its own design with its own outline, props and gait. A lobby never seats two of the same kind. New Look rolls everything, the kind included; there's no picker. Drawn clean and light: a thin, varied pen in a dark of the body's own color. Details: [CHARACTERS.md](CHARACTERS.md).
- The local player's **name, look and color persist** across sessions (localStorage). On first join the game asks for a name, with the random one offered as the one-click default. New Look rerolls the look and keeps the name and the color.
- **You pick your color.** The name screen (your name on your lobby card, Run on a pad in the lobby, or the join card) has the **player palette**: eleven swatches around the wheel, each tuned to read as a costume on every kind, on every theme and on the HUD. A pick shows on your character at once, there and for everyone in the room, and is saved with your profile. Another seat on the same screen (a pad beside you) gets the same screen for its own card; its name and color last as long as the seat. A newcomer starts in the free swatch nearest their roll. Profiles saved before swatches keep the color they had until they pick one.
- **Names are filtered** against published lists of slurs and obscenities (public/ASSETS.md → Word lists). Name entry doesn't take a refused name: the field goes back to the one you had, with nothing said. A refused name from anyone else shows as that player's generated name, and the lobby list leaves it off. Slurs are caught anywhere in a name, however it's dressed up (lookalike letters, digits for letters, stretched or spaced out); milder words only as whole words, so a town, a mushroom or a novelist with one inside stays allowed. Generated names never roll a refused one.
- **Everyone in a lobby looks different**: no two players share a kind, a name or a color, and their colors sit well apart. **One player per swatch**: a swatch someone seated wears (or that an older, rolled color sits too close to) shows as taken and can't be picked. Two players reaching for the same free swatch at once is settled by the host's order: the first gets it, the other keeps theirs and sees it taken. A saved color someone else is wearing when you sit down gives you the free swatch nearest it, and yours comes back when it frees.
- **Spawn zone is safe, for the first 5 s**: the starting zone is the protected rect around the spawn (the start pocket; every spawn's own in a mode with more), shown as a faint shimmer. For 5 s from GO, in every mode and every bout, while your body touches it, props and items can't hurt you: hazards don't kill, projectiles that reach you pop harmlessly, bonks and smoke don't reach you, and moving solids can't crush you. A faint bubble marks protected runners. Then it's gone for everyone: the shimmer and the bubbles blink through the last second and the zone is just ground, so nobody can camp in it (owner, 2026-11-06). The pit, the side edges, giving up and the race timer always apply. Placement is separate: parts can't be placed in a protected rect, whatever the clock.
- **Players are solid, and a head is a perch, not ground.** You land on a head from above, jump off it and drop through it with down + jump; it counts as ground for coyote time and the jump buffer, so a quick stack-hop is a skill trick. Otherwise it's its own physics (`PERCH` in `src/sim/collide.ts`):
  - **Nobody carries you.** You keep your own momentum, and a head moving out from under you leaves you behind. You follow it up and down, never sideways.
  - **The top is round and slick.** Your feet barely grip it. Off centre you slide off the side you're on, faster the further out: about 0.7 s from 1 px off, 0.4 s from 2 px, 0.2 s from 5 px. Dead centre on someone standing still you last 1 s, then tip off.
  - **Bucking.** A head rising fast (a jump, a spring, a bumper) throws you up and off, a little faster than it rises: a pop, not a lift. A head and rider parting at more than about 5.5 tiles/s (a hard stop, a turn, a run-up or a hard shove underneath) knocks you off the way you were going against it, with a small hop. A base walking off from under you knocks you off its back. Every throw has a small shove that clears the head.
  - **Bumping.** Jump up into the feet of anyone not riding your head, whether they stand on the level (through a board you jump up through) or are in the air, and they're bonked (stars, credited to you like any bonk) and knocked off sideways, while you carry on up through them. Off a ledge they hop clear; in the air they never leave faster upward than that hop, so a bump never lifts anyone. On a wide ledge it's a stumble; on a narrow one, or mid-jump, it's the ledge. A rider on your head is bucked instead, and a head rising slower than a jump (a lip, a lift) is still a step up. Bots that are behind bump whoever stands on a board just over their heads.
  - Being knocked off never hurts by itself. A hazard you land in kills as usual, credited to its owner, never to the runner underneath. A ceiling still stops a stack (everyone bonks, nobody is squeezed). Stacking to reach higher is a feature, a brief one, and the generator never counts on it: levels stay finishable alone. Being stood on never hurts.
- **Side contact blocks and shoves.** Walking into someone standing still moves you both at about a quarter of walking speed (a run-up shoves harder). Equal efforts stall, a stronger push wins by the difference, and being rear-ended never slows you down. Nobody is ever pushed into terrain. A hazard you're shoved or knocked into kills as usual, credited the usual way: the shover gets nothing. No wall slides or wall jumps off people.
  - **A mode can shove harder** (`RunRules.shove`, the same for everyone in its races; a race keeps the above). In a brawl a walk moves someone standing there at half a walk, twice a race's shove; whoever is shoved off a ledge goes out over the drop at the speed they were shoved, not straight down; and running into someone at about 11 tiles/s or more (a real run-up) when they aren't pushing back at you, standing or going your way slower, is a **body check**: they're bonked like a bump from below (stars and credit, so a kill soon after is yours) and thrown on ahead of you, a little faster than you came. Pushing back still holds your ground (equal efforts stall), only a fresh meeting checks, and someone already seeing stars is only shoved, so running on into them can't stun-lock them.
- **Nobody can hold a line.** Pressed against bodies for 0.75 s, you slip through them, so a one-tile tunnel or bridge can't be body-blocked and runners meeting head-on in a corridor both get through. Runners that already overlap (leaving the spawn pile, a pipe exit, squeezed together by a moving block) pass through each other until they've come apart, with no pop.
- **Nobody waits on someone who isn't there.** A player with no input at all (no button, no pointer moving) for 30 s of a pick, a place or a race with its timer off isn't waited for (a timer already ends the wait, and marks nobody AFK): they're handed a card from the crate, their part is skipped, or their runner gives up. Time before the phase began doesn't count, so a finished runner can watch the rest of a race. Once that happens they're **AFK** until their next input: handed a card at once, skipped, popped at GO, like a disconnected player. In the lobby it takes 60 s, and an AFK player doesn't hold up everyone else's Ready (somebody present still has to ready). Their card says AFK. Score moves on by itself, and anyone can continue from Victory, so neither waits on anyone. Online, an AFK player is moved to the watchers (below) when the seat is wanted: in the lobby once it's full and someone is watching, and mid-match once two rounds in a row have ended with them AFK, their seat freeing at the boundary. Any input asks for the seat back.
- **Who touches whom.** While the spawn shield lasts, runners inside the spawn zone don't touch anyone, so a pile can't block the start. Finished and dead runners touch nobody, and neither does anyone a rope or keg is holding. Frozen and dazed runners stay solid: you can stand on them and shove them. While it lasts, a stack on a glider weighs it down like that many riders: whoever is on a head still weighs on what that head stands on.

## Movement (precision-platformer feel)
- **Walk vs run.** Walking is slow and precise, about 6.6 tiles/s, with short jumps of about 4 tiles. Holding **Run** gives about 13.4 tiles/s and jumps of about 8 tiles, but run momentum builds over a beat, skids when you let go or turn, and carries you past ledges if you're careless.
- A full jump is about 3.7 tiles high and a tap about 1.3. Variable jump height (hold to go higher), coyote time, jump buffering, a little hang at the apex, and fast-fall (hold down in the air).
- **Wall slide and wall jump.** Holding into the wall while mashing jump climbs it; holding away kicks off hard.
- **One-way platforms**: jump up through them; down + jump drops through.
- Forgiving corner correction on ceilings and ledge lips.
- **The level's left and right edges are open, and leaving is death.** Past them is air, not a wall, so there's nothing there to slide down or kick off. A runner can lean out over an edge and step back; one who goes all the way off dies, like the pit, and leaves nothing behind. A level that wants a wall at its edge builds one out of terrain, and every spawn pocket has one: a wall at the left edge a row taller than a jump at the level's gravity, so holding left at GO, or a hop, stops against it. Climbing it on purpose is allowed.
- **Gravity is a level setting**: Auto (rolled per level), Low, Normal or High. Levels are validated and spaced at their own gravity: under low gravity even the easy gaps open wider, so a floaty jump still has something to cross. On Auto, space has its own gravity, floatier than Low (0.55 of normal: a full jump is about 6.6 tiles high and a second in the air); a host's Low, Normal or High still wins there. Runners and everything in flight fall at the level's gravity.

## Levels
- Every level has a **theme**: farm, factory, castle, jungle, volcano, harbor, mine, rooftops, glacier or space. The theme drives the terrain shapes, look, music and set pieces, so a farm reads as a farm. Glacier and space each come up on about 8% of seeds, the other eight on about 10.5% each; adding them moved no seed between the original eight.
- **A theme may change how you move, through the shared pieces only.** Its levels are still proven solvable with the real controller on that footing and at that gravity.
  - **Glacier** lays ice into its own terrain: the same surface an ice coat gives, so ice means one thing everywhere: long skids, a faster top speed once you build it, and a face you can't wall-jump. It goes in the beds, the long stretches of floor, and stops two tiles of rock short of any edge it drops off, so a skid ends on rock and not in a crevasse. The banks of a crossing that needs parts, the pockets, and anything too short to skate on stay rock; seracs, frozen falls and ice-cave roofs are ice right through. Ice is drawn as clear ice, so you can always tell it from rock, and no weather is drawn over it; rain or snow makes it slicker still, as with every slick surface. Any snow you see on a glacier is real snow weather.
  - **Space** floats: no ground and no floor to the pit, only docks, asteroids or station modules over the void, and falling off is death. Every gap is sized from jumps at space's gravity, so the voids are wide and the bodies small and few: the gaps between them are the long end of a walking jump, and a host's Normal or High packs them back in. That leaves no room for towers, so space levels are classic or wide. A classic space level asks for one part and a wide one more often for two. There's no weather in space.
- **A level set is a kit; the engine builds the level.** Each theme provides pieces with declared attributes (its ground, gap styles, stamps, tower floors and connectors, landmarks, hazards, decor), and each level shape or mode has one builder that composes any set's kit. A new mode is a new builder, and every set builds it.
- **Levels vary**: size and aspect (wide, classic, tall), sparse vs dense terrain, few vs many hazards, and route shape (sprawl, climb, descent, zig-zag).
- **Levels are mostly open.** Corridors are fine in places, but there's never only one narrow way to the goal: there are always route options, with hazards and choices along them. Measured on the real controller's routes with a level's required parts in place: take the fastest route and widen it by 2 tiles either side; a second route must stay out of that lane for at least 40% of the way. The spawn and goal pockets and the span of a bridge don't count, since nobody has a choice there. A level short of that gets raised lanes of jump-through platforms (carved galleries in a mine), and towers get two connectors between every pair of storeys. A tower's parts go on moats every route has to cross, on the goal storey and, for a second part, the spawn storey.
- **Precision is the point.** A crossing you jump asks for your range: a running jump or a walking one near its limit, and on all but gentle levels a share are the longest there is. Some are one-tile stones over the drop, each a walking jump from the last; too short to run on, so a running start off the bank carries you past the first. Platforms too short to run on are a walking jump apart, near its end. How much of each a level asks is its difficulty's.
- **Levels come in three difficulties: a fifth gentle, half medium, the rest hard.** A match deals a spread, not one flat mix. A level's difficulty is how many jumps on its more forgiving route a slip turns into a fall (letting go of jump early, jumping a beat late or early, running on after landing): none on a gentle level, at least one on a medium one, two or more on a hard one. The generator draws layouts until one fits, a few tries and then the nearest, so a seed keeps its theme, gravity and weather while its layout follows its difficulty. Harder levels also roll longer jumps, more stones, more crossings, near-full-jump steps in their landmarks, second routes of short perches, and more and nastier set pieces; gentle ones the reverse.
- **Levels need parts.** About 99% can't be finished bare, and 20–30% need two parts; the rest need one. What stops a bare run is a gap or a wall a part solves, never a sealed level. Every level must be finishable with **at most two small bridges**, verified with the real movement controller and never with hand-waved distances.
- The spawn and goal zones are protected: nothing can be built or bombed there.
- **The flag is generous**: its finish trigger is a bit wider than the flag and reaches 4 tiles up, so jumping clear over it still counts. Touching it ends your control: you drop, land and ride platforms with normal physics, and nothing can hurt you anymore.
- **Weather** is a per-level random variable, one kind per level, set by the seed and weighted per theme. About 75% of levels stay clear. Mines only ever get mist, rain and snow stay out of volcanoes and factories, and space never has any. Nothing on screen says what it does: you read it off the world.
  - **Anything you can see of weather does something, and nothing cosmetic pretends to be weather.** No theme or mood draws rain, snow, fog, falling ash or blowing leaves, and banners, grass, vines and flags hang still unless a gust is really coming or blowing.
  - **Wind**: level-wide gusts on a beat that's fixed per level, blowing one way for the whole level. They push runners on the ground and in the air, gliders, and every shot in flight. A gust beats a walk and loses to a run: stroll into one and you go backwards, and to stand your ground you run. A warning always runs first: 1.2 seconds of thickening wind lines, leaning grass and banners and a rising whistle, then the push. Between gusts the air is still and silent, and there's always more calm than the longest move the solvability proof uses. You can see the gusts come and go while you pick and place.
  - **Mist** limits sight: everyone has the same clear air around them, and there's a smaller clearing at the flag, so you know where to go but not what's in the way. Every live runner gets it, on every screen, so nobody sees further than anyone else. It's a thin veil while you pick and place, and closes in over the countdown. It's render only.
  - **Rain** keeps your speed and takes your brakes: long skids. **Snow** is a little slower and about twice as far to stop. Both are on everything you can stand on: terrain, one-ways, placed blocks, bridges, moving platforms, set pieces and gliders. Slick things get slicker, conveyors still run and jelly still bounces, and walls keep working. Wet ground shines, snow lies on every top, and it only falls when the footing is real.
  - **Weather never changes what a level needs.** It's chosen after the terrain and from its own roll, so the terrain, the solution and the share of levels needing parts are the same with or without it. The real controller then checks each pick: rain or snow has to leave the solution working and the level mostly open and still in need of its parts, and a gust can't do a part's job, even with the player choosing when to use it. A tailwind that would turns into a headwind, and a level that fails anything else stays clear.
- **The picture fills the window.** The play view keeps one aspect and scale, so nobody sees more of a level by resizing, and there are no bars or visible edge around it. The play view and any level shorter than it sit centered, so spare height goes evenly above and below. The sky and skyline run to the window's edges. Past the map's left and right edges there's nothing to stand on, so nothing is drawn there: the level's ground ends in its own face at the edge, and only the sky, the skyline and the pool go on, so out there reads as a drop, like a pit. A ceiling on the top row carries on up a little (a mine's rock all the way). Below the map the pool carries on down, and whatever stands on the bottom of the level runs on under its surface, so the pit still reads as death; in space nothing stands on anything, so nothing runs on. None of the scenery shows inside the play area. The scenery can't be reached, and corner UI stays in the window's corners.
- **The camera always frames every live runner**, local or remote, with a margin: it zooms out as far as it takes, down to the whole level, and catches up instantly if smoothing would let anyone leave the screen. Finished and dead runners drop out of the framing. The one exception is Rising Slag's leader's screen (Modes), where the screen is the arena and falling off it is how you go out.
- **Cel shading is the art direction**: flat tones, hard light bands, ink outlines, hard shadows. It is the one look for everyone, not a setting.
- **Render layers, back to front:** sky and skyline; background halves of set pieces (a windmill's tower, a crane's mast); liquids (water and lava); terrain, props and the moving parts of set pieces (the windmill's blades); players; effects. Scenery that you can't touch sits behind the terrain.
- Set pieces are large, pre-placed theme props: tractor, windmill, foundry press, chandelier, minecart, a mine roof that caves in and a runaway cart with its bell ringing, geysers, crane, a frost cannon, a chairlift, snowballs rolling off a cornice and thin ice that gives way on the glacier, a haunted manor's broken windows bristling with glass, a portcullis that slams on whoever stops under it, a bell swinging in its belfry, firebars turning over a bridge across a trench of lava, solar flares licking up out of the void, a centrifuge, meteors falling out of the sky, airlocks that blow you off the deck and fuel cells that go up after you land on them in space, and so on. They never block the known solution and keep their hazards away from spawn and goal. They are visible in every phase.

### Stage concepts
A level is a place with one landmark you'd name it after: the Great Wheel, the Haunted Wing (docs/SETPIECES.md has the plan and every concept).
- **About 80% of levels get a concept and about 20% stay plain**, where a concept fits the level's size and route. The concept is rolled once per level from its own stream. Plain levels are generated exactly as before concepts existed.
- **The landmark comes first.** The sculptor takes the landmark's site and builds its structure as tiles, so the real controller proves routes over it like any terrain. Its moving parts and hazards go in after weather, every one of them required; the filler set pieces after them come from the concept's own hazard family. A landmark that won't stand up is retried, and after 8 tries the level goes plain.
- **A level with a landmark is named after it**, for example "The Great Wheel of Skookaish Mill". Plain levels keep their place name.
- **Movers are never required.** A lift, a wheel or a cart is a fast or risky way, never the only one: the proof stays static, and every concept keeps a static way past itself.
- **Except a carrier, and then it's big** (decision 1a). A giant contraption may carry you over a gap no part could close: a lift or ferry parked at each end, across a chasm wider (or a climb taller) than two parts can manage at the level's gravity, and the contraption at least as big as the gap. It's proven conservatively: a runner who steps on while it's parked at one end and stands still is carried to the other, with nothing in the way, and steps off while it's parked there. Anything that check can't prove keeps a static way past. The carrier's gap isn't the level's part-requiring crossing: the level still needs its one or two parts elsewhere, and no mover, the carrier included, may let a bare run skip that crossing.
- **Every theme has at least 5 landmarks**, all large (a third of a level or more), and between one and three of them grand (they own most of the level, or it's built inside them). Every landmark varies with its seed: storey count, widths, where the doors, stairs and movers go, which hazards it carries, so no two Haunted Wings are the same building.
- **Every theme also has at least 12 decor pieces** (scenery that dresses the level: theme-specific, grounded, varied by seed, never in the way) **and at least 3 hazards unique to it** (world-only set-piece hazards no other theme uses, on top of the shared ones).
- **A landmark has more than one way through, and blocking one is play.** Every concept builds at least two genuinely different routes past itself (the Haunted Wing's stairs, hall and lift), with doorways and corridors a placed beam or block can shut. Shutting one to force rivals the long way is intended strategy. Shutting the last way is allowed too: then the crate deals a bomb.
- Triggers between pieces (a button that calls the lift) and changes between rounds are out of scope for now.

## Difficulty
- **One standard for every mode: a stretch of route has a tier, and a tier means how often a bot gets through it.** A stretch is a piece of route measured on its own (`Stretch`, `src/gen/level/difficulty.ts`). A race level is one stretch, from the spawn to the flag; a Rising Slag loop has one a lap. A race level's tier is what its precision asks (Levels: how many jumps on its forgiving way a slip turns into a fall), and the generator draws layouts until it lands in its tier's band, or takes the nearest; a loop is built to its level's tier the same way, and its laps' tiers are the slag's leash, since every lap climbs the same rows. In a Rising Slag shaft a slip that lands you two rows or more below where you jumped counts as a fall: with the slag rising, the climb lost is what costs.
- **The targets** (`PASS_TARGETS`, `src/game/expectations.ts`) are the share of stretches a lone bot of each level gets through:

  | Bot | Gentle | Medium | Hard |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | Easy | 75% | 50% | 30% |
  | Normal | 90% | 70% | 50% |
  | Hard | 95% | 85% | 70% |

  In a race, getting through is reaching the flag with the plan's bridges built (`tests/stats/bot-finish.test.ts`); in Rising Slag, climbing the lap before the slag catches you (`tests/stats/slag-climb.test.ts`). Each suite holds its mode to bands around what it measures today (`PASS_BANDS`), with the known gaps written beside them: a hard race level still plays little harder than a medium one, and in Rising Slag every lap passes above its target, since a slip on bricks lands a rise down, short of even the hard lap's leash. A new mode measures against the same targets; a mode that isn't about getting through (Brawl) adds its own row.
- **Catch-up** in Rising Slag is measured as how often whoever leads 15 s after GO wins the round, four Normal bots on a bare loop (`LEAD_HOLDS`, `tests/stats/slag-catch-up.test.ts`): 40% with the loop's boxes and Hand-Me-Downs as built, 44% when what the boxes deal reaches every empty hand and the bots use it, 52% when they hold it and never use it, with 3.0 lead changes a round (512 seeds; before Hand-Me-Downs 47%, 40%, 52% and 2.4; before the graded deal 52%, 43%, 52% and 2.3 with the climb's own items, the leader free, bots that see only the screen; 67, 45 and 68 and 1.5 with bots that saw the whole shaft; 53, 49 and 51 and 3.1 with the regroup gates, before the climb's items); the target, from the lead-camera racer, is about half (MODES.md). The same suite holds a round to 20–40 s (`SLAG_ROUNDS`) and prints who went out to what: 26% off the screen, 70% into the slag, 5% to hazards.

## Props
- **Not everything is death.** Most parts have an upside *and* a side effect you have to worry about: knockback, slow, freeze, sticky, misdirection, or a platform that sinks. Lethal parts exist, but disruption is the norm.
- **Some projectiles are platforms.** For example, gliders fired from a launcher can be ridden, but they sag and sink under weight or over time.
- **Demolition clears parts; changing terrain is rare.** Bombs, ring and cross charges, time bombs and EMPs destroy placed parts and leave the level's terrain alone. Only the drill digs terrain, plus a rare small mining bomb (one bomb in five). All respect spawn and goal protection.
- **The loot box** is one part with one look: a solid one-tile question-block cube. Hit it from below or bump its side and a random *held item* pops out of the top (out of the bottom under a low roof); any item can come out. Standing on it does nothing. A box either refills on a timer or runs out and goes dark. Which item pops is seeded per race, so a crate that survives a round doesn't repeat itself, and it's the same on every peer.
- **Held items** are carried one at a time, grabbed by touching them (contested items go to the nearest runner). The Use button fires them; down + Use tosses them. Loose items fizzle out after 10 s. Runners who die or finish drop theirs, except into the pit. Nothing carries over between rounds. **Items never kill on their own**: they bonk (a shove plus ~0.5 s of seeing stars, with no control), and a death soon after a bonk credits the bonker, like any trap kill. Every item has a catch:
  - **Master Noodle** (4 swings, a foam sword): bonks rivals and swats shots back, which makes them yours (rideable gliders and stuck patches are left alone). You crawl while you swing.
  - **Evicted Shell** (a kicked shell): slides along floors and banks off walls, and can come back to bonk its kicker.
  - **Stunnerang** (a boomerang): bonks the first rival it meets and curves back to your hand, wherever you've run to. If it hits anything else it clatters down for anyone to grab. A launcher's boomerang stays lethal.
  - **Bubble Wrap** (a pop shield): soaks one lethal hit and pops you upward, with a short guard after. It doesn't stop falls or crushes, and carrying it lowers your jumps. Use drops it.
  - **Free-Trial Jetpack**: one huge steerable burst upward, then the fumes daze you.
  - **Fog of War** (2 smoke bombs): a thick cloud that hides the ground and slows everyone inside, you included.
  - **Walk-Off Bat** (the brawler's home-run bat, 1 swing): Use winds up, and you're rooted until it lets go: press again to swing early, or it swings by itself at full charge (about 0.6 s). A tap knocks less than the noodle, a full charge about three times as hard. It swats shots like the noodle. A bonk mid wind-up throws the charge away, though you keep the bat.
  - **Tremor Brick** (the block that shakes the floor, 1 use): raised overhead for a beat, then slammed: every rival standing on something is bonked and bucked up. Anyone in the air is untouched, and so is anyone in the spawn zone or held by a part.
  - **Front-Runner Tax** (the leader-hunting winged shell, 1 use): flies over everything to whoever is nearest the flag by real route (not a straight line), hovers over them for a second with its shadow on the ground, then dives on the spot they stood and bonks everyone within two tiles. It retargets if the leader changes before the dive. If you lead, it comes for you. A dive into the spawn zone just pops.
  - **Pocket Block** (3 blocks): puts one solid turf block under your leading foot, ahead of you, or right under you in the air. It lasts 4 s, shakes, then crumbles. It never goes in a protected zone, on terrain or a part, or in a cell with anyone in it. Items may make solids mid-race like this; the solvability proof never counts on them.
  - **Queue Jumper** (the swap hook, 2 uses; a climb's loop boxes only): a velvet rope reels out to whoever is just ahead of you (on a climb, the nearest one above) and you trade places, laps, speed and all. It retargets if someone overtakes while it reels. Nothing blocks it. From the front it does nothing and you keep it.
  - **Sucker Punch** (a boxing glove on a spring in a gift box, 3 punches; a brawl's stands only): a swing with three times the noodle's reach that knocks almost twice as hard and lifts no more, so whoever it meets flies sideways into whatever is behind them. It swats shots like the noodle.
  - **Upper Hand** (a boxing glove on a firework, 2 punches; a climb's boxes and a brawl's stands): flies straight up through floors, leaning toward the nearest rival above slower than a walk, and punches everyone it reaches down and out. Step aside and it misses.
  - **Rude Awakening** (a twin-bell alarm clock, 2 lobs; a brawl's stands only): lobbed in an arc, a high one with up held, over a lip or up onto a ledge. Once down it rings, a dial the size of its blast sweeping round it, then goes off: every rival within three tiles with no rock between is thrown out from it and up. Its thrower is spared; the catch is the ring, long enough to step out of.
  - **Catch of the Day** (a fishing rod, 2 casts; a brawl's stands only): the line flies out straight ahead about ten tiles, or slanting up with up held, through boards but not rock, and yanks the first rival it snags toward you, off whatever they stood on: across a gap, into the drop between. The float stays hooked on them, the line reeling in with them, till they come down. It reels you in where every other item knocks you away. You stand rooted while the line pays out, and a bonk snaps it.
  - **Slippery Slope** (an oil can, 1 pour; a climb's boxes only): every ledge and wall in the twelve rows under your floor goes slick for 5 s, slicker than ice: long skids off narrow ledges, no wall jumps up the chimney. Your own floor is spared; slip back into it and it's slick for you too.
  - **Trickle-Down** (a money bag, 1 stamp; a climb's boxes only): a beat after the stamp a shower of falling coins pours down the shaft, fourteen rows (a line of dust marks where it stops), and everyone it meets standing on something or clinging to a wall is shaken off it, seeing stars: down through boards, or hopped off the nearer lip of a ledge. Anyone in the air as it passes is missed: you see it coming and jump.
  - **Social Climber** (a gold grapple on a string of pearls, 3 climbs; a climb's boxes only): hooks the highest ledge two to twelve rows overhead, up to three across, that it can reach with no rock in the way (straight up beside it and over the lip, or across and up through boards), and reels you up onto it in about a third of a second. With nothing in reach it does nothing and you keep it. A knock snaps the line.
- **Grab props** hold you and move your body in place of the controller; your hitbox doesn't change. A part holds one runner at a time, and a contested grab goes to the nearest runner, then the lower slot. There's a short cooldown before you can re-grab the part you just left, but any other part is fair game at once, so they chain. While held, fields, springs, wind and kickers skip you and other runners pass through you. Hazards, the pit, the flag and self-destruct all still apply. If the part goes away, a solid moves into you, or anything shoves you (a knock, a shield pop, a rocket), it lets go and you keep your speed.
  - **Sausage Vine** (the jungle-vine swing, on a butcher's hook): hangs from a ceiling or a placed block. Where there's no ceiling at all above it, it's lowered in on a string from open sky, so it can always be placed; it snaps a few rows up or down to reach a ceiling, and rotation does nothing. It falls with the ceiling it hangs from. You grab it automatically when you touch it in the air, wherever you touch it, and your speed becomes swing speed. Left and right pump the swing; jump lets go along the swing with a little extra fling, enough that a good release beats a running jump. **The catch:** it snaps if you hang on too long and grows back later. The links sear and thin first. Held items work on it.
  - **Kegapult** (the barrel cannon, as a moonshine keg): pulls you in on contact, on foot or in the air. Some hold a fixed aim and some tip between stops. Jump locks the aim and fires you after a short delay. **The catch:** a fuse burns down while you sit inside, and if it runs out the keg backfires, out of the rear and slower, and leaves you sooty. Held items stay in your pocket inside a keg. A shot that falls back into its own keg gets caught again.
  - **Y-Fling** (the slingshot, a keg mode): you sit in the pouch, visible, and point the stick where you want to go, any of eight directions, like every other control; the pouch draws back the other way on its own, from a weak flick to full power the longer you sit. Jump fires. Letting go of the stick keeps the aim and the pull, and a diagonal survives its two keys lifting a few frames apart. The keys you ran in with don't aim it until you press a direction afresh, so arriving keeps the placed aim instead of turning it round, and holding the aim through the flight carries the shot. **The catch:** sit in it too long and the band shivers, snaps, drops you, and takes a while to grow back. Held items work in the pouch.
- Props are assembled from **parts**: blocks of various materials, spikes, swinging heads, launchers, emitters, motion, springs, fans and bombs. A part's numbers are rolled once per match, and its name comes from them.
- **Each match has a roster, and the Parts rule sets how big.** The seed picks the parts, and every crate that match draws only from them. **Few** is the short list players learn by heart: 8 parts for 1–2 players and one more per player after that, up to 14 for 8 players, at least 4 more than a crate has cards, so crates differ round to round while every part keeps coming back. **Some** holds at least 12 (7 more than the cards), **Many**, the default, at least 18 (10 more), and **All** every part there is. A bigger roster starts with the smaller one, so the rule only adds parts, and so does a player joining between rounds.
  - Every roster holds the bridge part, a demolition part, a mystery crate, three hazards, a utility part and another building part. The rest are any other part, one of each kind.
- **Parody batch** (docs/PARODY-BACKLOG.md):
  - **Launch Plate** (the jump pad): a floor plate that *sets* your speed to its throw from its own center, so every launch flies the same arc whatever you came in with, and lands on a chalk X drawn where the real controller lands from it (if you don't steer; you still can). The place phase dots in the whole flight. It always lies on a floor: rotation picks the throw, right or mirrored left, flat and long or a higher, shorter lob.
  - **Four-Square** (the falling-block pieces): a shape a platform part can roll, one of the seven four-cell pieces in one colour each, the long one half as often. Plain footing that comes in one size, and never stands in for the bridge.
  - **Boost Strip** (the dash panel): an attachment like the coats. Touch it and you're held at about 1.6× run speed along its arrow for half a second, with no skid; its arrow points a quarter turn on from its face, so on a floor it runs right, under a ceiling left, up a wall's left face and down its right. On a wall it carries you along the face at that speed while you touch it. Weather doesn't stop it.
  - **Panic Button** (the P switch): a button and a dashed outline of 3–6 blocks. Press it (walk over it or bump it) and the blocks are solid for 5 s, blinking faster in the last second; pressing again restarts the time. A block never fills in around a runner: it waits until they're clear. The proof counts the blocks as gone.
  - **Fire Hydrant**: a cast-iron plug you can stand on, one tile, in red, yellow, green or blue. Its open outlet rattles, then blasts a jet of water for about a second that shoves whoever it catches along it, and nothing while it waits. Some have both side caps off and blast both ways. Rotation turns the jet: up launches whoever stands on it. The plug always stands upright. A utility part; the jet's reach is what scales.
  - **Fuse Crate** (the TNT crate): a crate you can stand on. Landing on it lights a 3-2-1 count; then it blasts everyone within about a tile and a half and is gone for a few seconds, coming back only when nobody's in its way. A shot, a flame, a laser, an arc or another crate's blast touching it sets it off at once, so crates chain. The blast clears no terrain and no props. It's a hazard: kills go to whoever placed it.
- **Variants differ in size only.** Within a match a part has one name, one look and one behavior; it comes small, medium or large, and a crate can show it at more than one size. Small and large cards say so in the name. Parts with nothing to scale (launchers, lasers, springs, launch plates, hangars, mystery crates, kegs, slingshots, fuse crates, Four-Squares) come in one size. Cards draw every size of a part at one scale.
- **A fresh level can always be finished; parts can seal it, and a bomb is the way out.** A plan is at most two bridges, each a spot the bridge card can go, with which the real controller reaches the flag over terrain and plain blocks while keeping clear of every other part (anything a part holds for most of its cycle: hazards, coats, springs, fans, slippery or moving blocks), with time bombs counted as gone off. A level starts from its recorded solution, which every set piece must leave standing. Placements are never refused for leaving no way through: parts can seal the level, and a round nobody can finish scores nothing and moves on. Each round's crate is dealt for the level as it stands: at least one hazard and as many medium bridge cards as the plan still has bridges, never fewer than one; when there's no plan, the level is sealed and the crate deals a demolition part that clears placed parts (a bomb, a charge or a time bomb), a hazard and one bridge card. Every roster holds such a part. Past those cards no card repeats. After rounds where nobody finishes, crates quietly lean toward bombs, building parts and springs on the roster, gently after one dry round and more from the second, up to a cap. Crate contents are tied to the level seed and round, but the card order is shuffled with a per-match salt the host rolls, so it differs every game.
- **Secret Parts** (a rule) hides the parts. The crate deals role cards instead (Build, Hazard, Utility, Demolish), each an inked card in its role's color with its label and nothing else, in the same mix of roles the crate would have dealt, dry-round lean included. Each is still claimed by one player, first come first served. Claiming one rolls the part right then, from the match roster filtered to that role, weighted like the crate and seeded with the claimer and the frame: the same on every peer, and nobody can know it in advance. The part is revealed when place starts, to everyone: the claimer needs to see what they're placing, a shared couch screen can't hide one player's ghost from the rest, and seeing what the others drew is the payoff of the gamble. The crate guarantee holds: the role card standing in for each bridge card is a build card, and it rolls the bridge while the plan still needs one, so claiming every build card always yields the bridges. Mystery crates and held items are unchanged.
- **Placement is on the tile grid; a part's solid shape needn't be.** A part takes whole tiles to place, and those cells are its for placing and for the plan. Inside them its solids can be any shape (a pipe's lip standing proud of its shaft), as long as the art matches them (docs/ART.md, fair play), you can stand on every top it shows, and no gap the shape leaves in its cells can catch or snag a runner. A sub-tile step a part means you to walk over climbs like a ledge lip. Rounded shapes are allowed the same way: the solids step to follow the curve to within about a pixel, every step low enough to walk or jump round, so a round rim is as round to stand on as it looks.
- **A part takes the tiles its object does.** Most are one tile, but nothing has to be: a launcher whose object is long or tall takes two (`LAUNCHER_FRAMES`). The hangar is its hangar and the runway its planes roll out along, and the pirate cannon its carriage (2×1); the ballista stands on its tower, the vending machine, the tiki post and the gumball machine stand tall (1×2). Machines whose object is a squat block (arrow trap, turret, mortar, sentry, jets, emitters) stay one tile. A bigger part is harder to fit and blocks more, which is the trade for reading as what it is: a tall one is a wall you wall-jump, a long one a ledge you climb. Its shots leave from where its art shows the muzzle.
  - **Machines stand upright.** A quarter turn turns a launcher onto a wall, but a half turn mirrors it: it faces the other way on the same footing, its sub-tile steps mirrored, and a lobber still lobs (`Xform.U`).
- **Parts pass through each other.** A part goes anywhere its tiles are free of terrain, protected zones and whatever stands there right now; the path of a mover or the swing of a set piece takes nothing, and the mover passes through what's in its way. A moving part's own path must still keep out of rock and protected zones. A runner caught between a mover and a part isn't crushed: the mover passes through them. A rider carried into a part is knocked off, like hitting a ceiling, and drops through what carried it. Only the world, or a crusher's head, crushes.
- **A red ghost shows what's in the way**: every tile that refuses it gets a red cross. Confirming a red ghost buzzes and places nothing.
- **Every part can always be placed somewhere**, or a pick could stall the place phase on a level without the right surface.
- **Attachments** (spike strips, ice coats, slime coats, snap traps) must mount flush on an exposed face of a solid block, and rotation picks the face.
  - A strip too long for its spot still places: only the cells that can mount land, the rest are gone for good, and the ghost shows exactly what lands.
  - Ice makes floors slippery and walls slick (no wall jump).
  - Slime makes floors slow and walls sticky (you cling).
- **Conveyors have one belt face, and rotation picks it**: top, right, bottom or left, with the belt running the same way round the block, so a belt carrying you right on top runs down the right face, left along the bottom and up the left face. Every other face is a plain block.
  - A top belt carries walkers.
  - A side belt grips a runner clinging to it (holding into it in the air) and carries them at its own speed: running up, it's an escalator that lifts you over its top edge; running down, it lowers you. It never slows a jump that's already rising faster than it. Wall jumps work as usual, and a belt running up adds its speed to the jump; one running down takes nothing away.
  - A bottom belt flings a head that bonks it sideways along it at twice its speed, about a run. That keeps the underside a consequence you see coming, without a new verb like clinging under it.
  - The plan keeps clear of conveyors at every rotation, like any part that isn't plain footing.
- Bombs clear terrain and/or props depending on their kind. Attachments fall off when their host block is destroyed.
- **Parts ride what they're stuck to.** A block (any material, a spike block, a mystery box) placed flush against a moving solid, or a coat or spike strip mounted on a moving face, becomes that mover's rider and follows it for the rest of the match. A coat on a lift is a slime lift; a block on a wheel's car is a step that goes round with it.
  - **Translation only.** Physics is axis-aligned, so a rider follows its mover's travel and never turns: a coat on a wheel's car rides round with the car and keeps its face, as the car itself stays level.
  - **What carries:** the blocks of a part that moves (a moving platform, a lift, a track car), a wheel's or pendulum's cars, and a cart's body, set pieces included (a slime coat on the Great Wheel's cars, spikes under a lift). Not a crumbling block (it breaks and would leave its riders floating), a blink block (half the time it isn't there), a crusher (when it moves depends on who walks under it, so the proof can't follow it), a swinging blade or a rotating saw (no solid to stick to), runners, or gliders.
  - **What rides:** blocks, mystery boxes and coats, things that hold still and move as one piece. Parts that aim, cast or hold a runner from a fixed mount (launchers, springs, fans, ropes, pipes, snap traps) and anything with motion of its own place by the usual rules.
  - **Chains:** a coat on a block that rides a lift rides the lift too.
  - **Placing.** Movers keep moving while you place, so a part sticks to where its mover is on the frame it goes in: within three quarters of a tile it snaps flush against the moving face. The ghost rides along, shows the loop it will travel as a dotted line with a bead running round it, and wears a chain link where it touches what carries it. Touching a mover means riding it: a part whose ride would take it into rock, off the level's side or into a protected zone anywhere along the way is refused, never placed still beside the path. A coat goes to whichever wins more of its cells, the still faces or one mover, and the still faces win a tie.
  - **Bombs.** A rider goes with what it rides: blast the mover and everything stuck to it goes too, chains included; blast a rider and only it and what rides it go. Nothing falls and nothing is left floating. A blast hits a rider where it is when the blast goes off.
  - The plan counts a rider wherever its mover takes it, like any mover; it's never footing the plan stands on.
- **Movers and traps with a catch.** Warped pipes (below), bumpers (aimable kick), gravity wells / repulsors (bend jumps and drag you along the ground), webs (catch falls, slow you), blink blocks (solid on a timer, pairs alternate, never reappear inside you), cog wheels (ride platforms), dropstones (slam when you pass under, ride them back up) and snap traps (face-mounted spikes that pop when you linger). Every timed or triggered part shows a tell first.
- **Warped pipes** (the plumber's warp pipe) only work in pairs.
  - Each pipe is its own card and its own placement, taking a footprint two tiles across and 2, 3 or 4 tiles long (its small, medium and large). Its solid is a shaft a tile and a half across with a lip the full two tiles across and half a tile deep at the mouth end, so the lip stands out a quarter tile on each side. The lip is a rolled rim, round at every corner, and the shaft's far corners are softened; the solids step to follow both. Rotation picks the way the mouth faces. You stand on the lip and on the shaft's far end like a block, and walk over a sideways lip's quarter-tile step without jumping.
  - Pipes link in the order they go in over the match: the 1st with the 2nd, the 3rd with the 4th. An odd one waits for a partner, its mouth a dead black hole, and is only a block. A pair stays a pair while both stand. When a bomb takes one, its partner waits again and pairs with the next pipe placed, or at once with a pipe already waiting, oldest first. So there's never more than one pipe waiting. Links are sim state, set as parts go in and come out.
  - Going in: on a linked mouth facing up, stand over the opening and hold down. Under one facing down, hold up (or jump) with your head against it. At a sideways mouth, push into it, on foot or in the air. You slide in (a third of a second), come out of the partner's mouth facing its way (another third), and leave it with a hop, a walk or a drop, whichever way it faces. If the far mouth is blocked when you get there, the pipe spits you back out of the near one. Whatever would send you straight back into the mouth you came out of (down, out of one facing up) has to be let go and pressed again first.
  - A trip is a hold, like a keg: your hitbox waits just outside the mouth you're at, so a hazard there still gets you, other runners pass through you, and your held item stays in your pocket. One runner per pipe at a time. Only runners travel: shots and loose items never go through.
  - One pipe part per match, so every pipe that match is one material, rolled with the part: classic green, brass, rusty iron, porcelain or verdigris copper. A crate can show more than one pipe card, at different sizes. While a pipe waits for its partner, crates (and Secret Parts rolls) weight the pipe 4 times as heavily.
  - Pipes are utility parts. The winnability plan keeps clear of a pipe and its mouth like any part that isn't plain footing, so a match never relies on one, and the crate guarantee never counts one as a bridge.
  - Linked pairs wear a matching numbered job tag, in place and in the race, and a linked mouth breathes a faint draft. Both ends flare as someone goes through. While you place a pipe, a dotted line ties it to the pipe it would pair with.
- **Blood is slippery.** A runner who dies on or near a surface oozes a short puddle of their own body color along the floor under them (or a smear down a wall with no floor near), and it's ice underfoot for everyone, them included. Walls keep their wall jump. By default it's gone at the end of the next round (spilled in round R, it clears when R+1 ends), and the Blood Rounds rule sets that to 1, 2 or 3 rounds, or off for none at all. A new death on the same spot renews it. Pits leave nothing, protected zones stay clean, and it only lands on terrain and plain still blocks, so coats, conveyors and the like keep working. A blast clears every cell it touches, and blood goes with its block or tile, or under a block or coat placed over it.
- **Status effects are plain, short and visible**: frozen (no control, ~0.8 s, ice block) and slowed (capped speed and jumps, ~2 s, goo drips). The spawn zone's shield (its first 5 s) keeps them off you, and fields and kicks, as it does hazards.
- **Launcher aim is legible.** Aim rolls fixed 7 : sweep 2 : track 1.5, so most launchers hold a fixed direction. Trackers only watch ±60° of their mount, always carry a tracking word in their name, show a red sensor camera, and crate cards and the codex state the aim (Fixed, Sweeps, Tracks).
- **Sentry guns** (the team shooter's engineer sentry, our own tripod gun): a squat turret that sweeps its cone until a runner is in range, inside that cone and in its sight. Then it beeps, its lamp turns red, and it turns after them no faster than its turn rate, so a runner close in can outrun it. After a short spin-up behind a dotted laser sight, it fires one burst of fast rounds along its barrel, then cools down with its lamp amber. **Its rounds kill**, like every launcher's (items are what bonk), and stop at terrain and solids like every shot. Breaking its sight before the spin-up ends stops the lock. Between bursts it always holds fire longer than a route needs to slip past (the same opening timed hazards give), so it never seals a level, and the plan treats it like any launcher: only its block is in the way. It comes small, medium and large, and only its range differs.
- **Projectiles vary.** Only one kind homes: the seeker, which is rare, slower than a run, has a blinking red seeker lens and is tagged HOMING on its card. The rest: boomerangs, rubber balls (bounce; shove, don't kill), cluster shells (split at the top of the arc), goo and frost (non-lethal; slow or freeze you, coat open terrain in slime or ice for a few seconds, never inside a protected zone or a one-tile gap), ricochets (bank off walls) and **gliders** (a launcher fires one-way planks you can ride: they fly level, then sink, faster the more riders they carry, and can never pin you).
- **Demolition kinds**: bomb (disk, everything), EMP (props only), drill (terrain-only bore in the rotation direction, leaving props alone), cross and ring charges, and time bombs (go off as the race starts, so they catch whatever was placed that round; a charge caught in an earlier blast is defused). The placement ghost shows every affected cell and outlines the props that will go.
- **Solids stop whatever travels.** Flames, lasers, geysers, fan columns, shots, crushers and tracker sight stop at terrain and at any block that's solid on that frame, so a block is a shield; a tesla arc needs a clear line between its coils. One-way platforms, gliders and a part's own body don't stop them, and runners never do: a beam goes through everyone in it.
- **Updrafts are steady**: inside a fan column you rise or are pushed at a constant speed, standing in a column never stops you from jumping, and the visuals match exactly when it pushes.

## Rules ("level settings", set by the host)
- Match Length: default Normal. See Scoring.
- Pick timer: default 15 s. When it runs out, stragglers get a random leftover prop.
- Place timer: default 15 s. When it runs out, an unplaced prop is forfeited.
- Race timer: default off.
- Bouts per Pick: 1, 2, 3 or 5 races on each placement before the next pick. Choosing a mode sets the mode's own (Brawl 3, the others 1), and the host can change it after. A bout starts fresh on the same level and parts: everyone at their spawn, the slag down, the boxes and stands full, set pieces and parts as a race starts them. Only what a round carries over (scores, grudges, parts, blood) carries over.
- Gravity: default Auto.
- Weather: default on. Off makes every level clear.
- Blood rounds: how many rounds a puddle lasts, default 2. Off means no blood at all.
- Parts: Few, Some, Many or All, default Many: how many different parts the match's crates deal from. See Props.
- Secret Parts: default off. See Props.
- Lobby: Public or Private, default Public, online only. Private keeps the lobby off the lobby list. Never carried over to the host's next lobby.
- A rule the level is built from (gravity, weather, difficulty, and the mode, on its own plate) rebuilds the lobby's level on the spot, for everyone.
- **The Level card** opens from the level's plate in the lobby's top bar (New Level beside it still rolls a new one). It shows the level as it plays, with arrows either side that turn through the themes and **Any** (the seed's own theme); the theme and the level's name sit under the picture with a dice for a random theme. Below that are the **seed**, which you can type (a number or a word, read as a link's `?seed=`) or roll with a dice, and the **difficulty**: Grab Bag (the default: each level rolls its own, see Levels), Sunday Stroll, Fair Fight or Pain Train, the match's rule for every level. Every change rebuilds the level on the spot. A chosen theme stays for the levels after it. Online it's the host's, like the rules.
- The Rules card is one screen with no scrolling at 720p: a label and a row of choices per rule, no explanations.

## Lobby
- **A match starts from Start, never by itself.** Once everyone here is ready and there are as many as the mode needs, a molten **Start** shows under the bays, at the room panel's end online, and the lobby waits for someone to press it: offline anyone seated on this screen, online the host alone. A ready player's confirm presses it (the footer's confirm prompt reads Start); their card's Ready! still un-readies on a click or tap, and anyone un-readying (or a newcomer sitting down) takes it away again. A guest sees everyone ready and nothing to press. Start is a host action, so every peer starts on the same frame, and after a host leaves the lobby the next host's Start is the one that counts. A lobby with no person in it (only bots, or only bots and the AFK) starts by itself the moment everyone is ready, so tools, tests and bot trials need nobody.

## Controls and UI
- A picked part's card leaves the crate entirely. The other cards stay where they are.
- On PC, keyboard and mouse both work. One keyboard player can use WASD *and* the arrows. A "Split keyboard" setting turns the keyboard into two local players. Everything can be remapped.
- The mouse drives the menu cursor: click to pick or place, right-click or the wheel to rotate. They do nothing else, so a stray click can't self-destruct.
- **Self-destruct is a deliberate hold on every input**: alt (R or Q, B or LB, the 💥 button on touch) held for 3 s (`POP_HOLD`). A ring fills around your runner for everyone to see, and on your own screen a big 3, 2, 1 counts down in the middle; couch players charging at once share it side by side, each in their color. Remote players' charges show only the ring. It starts only on a fresh press during the race, and letting go, steering, jumping or using an item ends it; the next try needs a new press. So a hold carried over from place (where alt rotates) or a thumb resting on a button never counts. The sim counts the hold from the held alt in each frame's input, so every peer pops you on the same frame.
- A gamepad's cursor ramps up while the stick is held: a tap moves it a little, a long push crosses the screen fast.
- **Touch (mobile)**: left half is a floating stick (push past ~70% to run), right half is jump (hold for height), self-destruct is a small 💥 button under Settings, and a Use button appears above jump while you hold an item. Pick by tapping a card; place by dragging a ghost that floats above your finger, with ROTATE and PLACE buttons. Landscape first.
- **Phones see the game closer up**, and it stays render-only: a phone's camera is its own, and cursors stay in world coordinates so every peer sees them land in the same place. Solo, the race camera follows you with a tile at least ~24 CSS px, with look-ahead and a vertical dead zone, clamped to the level. **With more than one player in the match, local or remote, every live runner stays on screen, and that always beats the tile size**, except in Rising Slag, where the screen is the leader's and falling off it is going out. Pick and place start zoomed out, with pinch-zoom, two-finger pan, and auto-pan when a ghost nears an edge. HUD text, buttons and crate cards scale up, and every tap target is at least 44 CSS px.
- In the lobby, a stray click does nothing; only buttons act.
- UI copy is proper title case, with no twee lowercase taglines.
- Inputs are shown as **button prompts** (keycap, gamepad and mouse glyphs) in a footer, the way console games do it, and are never spelled out in prose.
- Settings are tabs, one screen each with no scrolling at 720p (on phones, a full-screen sheet with big rows that scrolls): Audio, Video, Keyboard, Mouse, Gamepad (Touch replaces the last three under touch). The same menu opens on the home screen and in-game, and works with mouse, touch, keyboard (Q/E tabs, arrows) and gamepad (LB/RB tabs, Start opens, B closes). It reopens on the last tab of that page load. Reset Controls shows on the Keyboard tab only, since nothing else it resets is on screen.
- Empty lobby slots show **OPEN**.
- **Music is a tune, not a loop.** Each level's song is 32 bars in its theme's style, with the melody on top of the mix. A theme has more than one band, all of them true to it (a farm plays a harmonica reel or a fiddle jig, a mine its tubular bells or a minecart western, a volcano its horns and drums or a boss fight), and the seed picks the band, key, mode, tempo and tune. Acoustic instruments are recordings from CC0 sample libraries, so a fiddle sounds like a fiddle; synth styles stay synths. The music leans dark: most themes favor minor modes. The menus, lobby list, codex and Settings play one fixed title tune, the foundry at night in a minor key; a lobby crossfades to its level's song, and leaving brings the title tune back.

## Online
- The URL `/r/<code>` identifies a lobby; the homepage is a normal main menu. There's no matchmaking: you join through a shared link, by typing its code under Join a Lobby, or from the lobby list.
- **Lobbies** (on the main menu) lists the public lobbies: each row shows the level's theme, the code, seats taken of 8, how many watch, and where the room is (Lobby, Picking, Racing…). **Join** lists rooms with a seat; full rooms are under **Watch**. It refreshes while it's open. Choosing a room is opening its link: the joining screen, then seated in a lobby with a seat, watching with Join mid-match, and watching from the start under Watch. Lobbies are **public by default**; the host can make one **Private** in the rules, which takes it off the list at once (invite link or code only). Every new lobby starts public. A room drops off the list when its last player leaves, or within a minute if its host vanishes.
- **Arriving.** Opening a room shows its code while the link comes up, with a token for each peer already there, then drops you in. If it can't (Online Not Set Up, Room Is Full, Cannot Reach Host, Different Version, Kicked) it says which, with Play Offline (except when kicked) and Main Menu. An empty room's link just starts that room: there's no "not found".
- **Seats.** Arriving in the lobby seats you without pressing anything, up to 8 players. Arriving while a match runs, or in a full lobby, you **watch**: the live match, with the race camera framing everyone and every cursor and ghost in pick and place, and nothing to control but the menus. On a phone that's no touch controls, just the view. A `?watch` link arrives watching wherever the match is.
- **Join** is a primary button in the bottom corner for anyone watching, in every phase, with the confirm key or button on it. With no seat free it reads **Match Full** and does nothing, and it lights up the moment one frees. Mid-match it opens the **join card** over the live match: your character big on a lobby card, the New Look dice (the toy is the dice's: there's no kind picker), your name and color to change, the seats as they stand, and Join. Cancel goes back to watching. Between rounds the seat is yours at once; mid-round the card says **You're In Next Round** and you're seated at the round boundary. **In the lobby, Join just seats you**, the way arriving does: no card, your own lobby card appears among the players with the lobby's own sound, and it already has the dice, the name and the color. Losing the last seat to someone quicker there says **Seat Taken** and Join reads Match Full.
- **The host decides a race for the last seat.** A Join is a request the host puts in order with everything else, so when two ask for one seat the first it hears wins, on every peer alike. The other's card says **Taken**, they go back to watching, and Join reads Match Full. A seat won mid-round counts as taken for anyone asking after. A second click is the same request; a request in flight when the host goes is decided by the new host, once; a winner who leaves before the boundary gives the seat back.
- **Gone players.** A player whose peer drops mid-match (a closed tab, a dead phone, a lost connection) is **away** at once on every screen: an unplugged icon on their race chip, score row and lobby card (AFK is a tag; gone is the icon), and their runner pops. Back within **40 s**, they carry on in their seat. Gone longer, they're dropped: their seat is free from then (Join lights up for watchers) and they leave at the round boundary. Their parts stay in the level. If they come back later they watch, and a Join seats them with their look and points as they were.
- **Rooms end.** A room nobody is in, players or watchers, for **60 s** is over: its link starts a fresh room under the same code, on the code's own level. Before then, the first one back picks the match up where it was. "Active" means this, here and in any list of rooms.
- **The round boundary** is the score screen: seats won mid-round are taken there, in the order they were won, and a seat freed mid-round (below) frees there.
- **Watch** is on your own lobby card (the eye beside the dice, unless a phone's bay is too narrow for it) and in the Settings menu, never a stray key. In the lobby it frees your seat at once. Mid-match you play the round out, the corner says **Watching Next Round**, and your runner leaves as the round is scored. Choosing Join again before then keeps the seat.
- **Watchers in the lobby** are a row of small chips in the online panel: an eye and a name each (AFK tagged), yours outlined. The host kicks a watcher from its chip, like a player from their card, in the lobby only.
- The host wears a `HOST` label in the lobby, and it moves with the host; a remote player's card shows their ping in the same spot, and there's no separate list of names. The host can kick a player from the lobby only, by mouse, or with Use, then Confirm on keyboard or gamepad. **A kick keeps that browser out for 60 s**, however it comes back: a reload, a new tab, the invite link. The kicked player sees Kicked with "You can rejoin in 0:59" counting down and Main Menu; at zero, Rejoin. Trying the link again inside the minute shows the same screen. The host can't lift a ban early.
- **Host = the earliest-joined connected player.** When the host leaves, the next player in join order takes over seamlessly, never a watcher while a player is here: a watcher steps to the back of the line. A host who starts watching hands the room to the next player. When only watchers are left, the earliest of them hosts until someone takes a seat. When everyone leaves, the next arrival becomes host. A refresh is a new join: mid-match you get your slot, look and score back, in the lobby your look, a watcher stays a watcher, and a host who refreshes comes back as a guest.
- Netcode sends inputs, not state, over a deterministic simulation that must be bit-identical across browsers. Snapshots are only for joining, resync and host migration. The host is the decider: when a guest's state drifts, the host's state wins.
- Peers connect over WebRTC in a star around the host. A guest that can't reach the host directly (and has no TURN server) drops to local play. Details: `docs/NETCODE.md`.
- In pick and place everyone sees everyone's cursor and held part live. A card belongs to whoever the host hears first; nobody sees a claim that gets taken back.

## AI players
Built-in bots race, pick and place, hold grudges (TODO 27), and move with the room when the host changes; spite in the race is next, then player scripts. The lobby's host adds a bot from an open bay (**Add Bot**) and sends it away with **Remove**; a bot readies itself.

- **Levels and personality.** Each bot has a level, Easy, Normal or Hard, set on its card by whoever runs it and shown on everyone's. Inside its level it rolls its own personality from its seed: how long it takes to see what's next after landing, how often it fumbles a jump, how much more it chokes on a tight one, how soon it saves a fumble in the air, how often and how long it stops to think, how often that ends on a worse line, how much room it keeps from hazards, how long it will wait for one to pass, and how long it takes over the crate. Two Normal bots play differently; an Easy one dithers and fumbles, a Hard one is quick. It never sees more or moves better than a player could at any level.
- **No bot is unbeatable.** Every level fumbles the way a level's difficulty counts slips (let go early, pressed a beat late or early, carried on through the landing), and on a tight jump, one a slip turns into a fall, its nerves pick the fumble that drops it, more often with a rival at its heels. Before a tight jump, or where a hazard made it weigh its moves, it may stand and think, less where it has thought before in the level, and a pause now and then ends on a worse line. Harder bots save a fumble sooner, doubt less and see the forgiving way better (it takes a safe move over a tight jump when the safe one costs little more); they never stop fumbling. Alone with the plan built, a bot should finish about (gentle, medium, hard level): Easy 75, 50, 30 in a hundred; Normal 90, 70, 50; Hard 95, 85, 70 (`tests/stats/bot-finish.test.ts` has where they stand). Harder bots are harder mostly by spite, not by perfect hands: a Hard bot acts on a grudge most rounds and picks the spot that hurts, an Easy one now and then and clumsily.
- **Bots get in the way.** Grudge or none, Normal and Hard bots try to make the run harder for everyone: once someone holds the plan's bridge, a blocking bot takes a hazard or a block and puts it on the way everyone will run, weighing a few spots there for the one that leaves the forgiving way longest and tightest. It won't add more than one tight jump (it has to make it too), never breaks the way the plan builds, and after a race nobody finished it leaves the level be. Normal blocks most rounds, Hard nearly every round and weighs the most spots, Easy now and then. In four-bot matches it takes Hard from 3.0 finishers a race to 2.3, and Normal from 2.5 to 1.8.
- **Temperaments and grudges.** Apart from its level, each bot rolls a temperament from its seed, never shown: players find out who's who by how it plays. A **builder** plays its own game and forgets a slight by the next round; a **gremlin** takes hazards for the fun of it and puts them wherever anyone ran, its own way included; a **grudger** remembers who hurt it for rounds; a **sheriff** goes after whoever leads on points; a **speedrunner** takes the quick, risky line and lets slights go. A bot holds a grudge only for what a player would have seen: a rival's part killing it, a rival's part in the way it ran, a rival leading, a rival taking the card it was reaching for. Grudges cool each round. Out for someone, it goes for the card they're pointing at or takes a hazard, though never the last card that builds or clears the way through (with nobody finishing, nobody scores), eyes their cursor a while before placing, and puts the hazard where they died or ran last race, at a spot that still leaves a way through when it's sharp. Grudges live on the machine running the bot, so a bot that moves to a new host starts with none.

- **A strategy per mode.** Picking and placing are the same in every mode; how a bot races is its mode's strategy (`STRATEGIES`, `src/brain/strategies/`), reading the objective the mode gives it (reach the flag, or climb). A new mode has no bots until it has one.
- **Bots use their items.** Each item has a tactic (`ITEM_TACTICS`, `src/brain/tactics.ts`), judged from what a player sees: the jetpack when the slag is close or it's stuck and there's a ledge above to land on, the Pocket Block underfoot at the top of a jump for the same reasons, the winged shell only from behind, the boomerang at a rival in range across (high at one above), the shell kicked along a floor at a rival with nothing between, the noodle and the bat at a rival beside it, smoke lobbed a few tiles onto one, the Tremor Brick when a rival stands on something, the Queue Jumper from anywhere but the front, the Upper Hand at a rival above and about overhead, the Slippery Slope from a floor while a rival climbs the ledges under it, the Trickle-Down while a rival below stands in its reach, the Social Climber on a ledge higher than a jump goes. A Leg Day a few tiles off its way is worth the step whatever it holds. Bubble Wrap is never let go. How quickly a bot sees its moment is its `knack`: Easy misses most, Hard few. With empty hands it goes out of its way, a few tiles at most and fewer as the slag closes in, for a loose item or a box whose pop it can reach (or that hands it over). Round a loop each bot climbs toward a column of the lap line of its own, a new one each lap, so a pack spreads over the shaft.
- **How a bot runs.** It follows the route the solvability proof would take, playing the controller's own moves, running along the ground, and waits out a hazard, a shot, a moving part or a gust that its next move would meet, but only as long as the wait pays against a clear way round. It looks one move ahead, so it won't drop onto ground that's about to flood or be swept with no way off, and when waiting it brakes to a stop it can make and leans into a gust. It doesn't know its way past what the proof doesn't model (riding movers, timing a door): with no way on, it runs and jumps for the flag anyway, and when even that gets it nowhere it self-destructs, as a stuck player would.

- **A brain is an input device.** It fills a seat and produces that seat's `InputFrame`s, nothing else. It runs on one machine: the host for built-in bots, the owner's own machine for a player's script. Its inputs cross the network like a human's, so the sim never runs brain code, lockstep and fairness stay as they are, and a brain doesn't have to be deterministic. That is what makes player scripts possible: nobody ships or sandboxes untrusted code on every peer.
- **A brain never cheats.** One hitbox, one physics model, the same inputs as anyone. In pick and place it moves a cursor and clicks like a player, so everyone watches it choose.
- **A brain rebuilds from what it sees.** When the host leaves, or steps back to watch, its bot seats move with the room and their brains start fresh on the new host, so a brain may keep memory but can't depend on it. A host who watches gives up only its own seat, never its bots'.
- **Senses, mind, motor.**
  - *Senses* are a versioned, read-only `BrainView`, not raw sim state, so scripts survive sim changes: yourself (position, velocity, footing, wall contact, held item), tiles and surfaces, props as tagged shapes and who owns each, other runners, the goal and the clock, and the table as everyone sees it: scores, who took which card, where each cursor points, whose part killed whom. Helpers: the reach route to the goal, `canPlace`, and a budgeted lookahead that steps a copy of your body.
  - *Senses give an idea of values, not the literal values* (leaning, not final). Positions come rounded to a fraction of a tile, speeds and timers as bands (still, walking, running, falling fast; a saw about to come round), and the lookahead answers with an outcome (lands on that ledge, falls, gets hit) rather than coordinates. A brain reads the game the way a player does, so a script can't play frame-perfect off exact numbers. Built-in brains see the same view, so a script competes with them on equal terms. The motor is the game's own code and acts on the real state, as a player's hands do.
  - *Mind* decides: which card, where and how to place it, and per frame either raw buttons or an intent (`goTo`, `jumpTo`, `wait`, `use`).
  - *Motor* turns intents into frames by replaying the controller policies `src/sim/reach.ts` already uses to prove routes, and turns a pick or placement into cursor moves and a click.
- **Built-in brains** are registry entries: a route-following planner over the reach graph that replans around hazards and runners, plus pick and place with taste. Difficulty, personality and temperament are data rolled from a seed: reaction (how stale its view is), precision (input jitter, jump timing), risk (fast line or safe line), spite (how often and how well it acts on a grudge) and what it holds a grudge for. Each bot gets a generated name. With a seeded `Rng` they're also playtesters: headless bot matches can measure finish rates and how parts get used.
- **Player scripts** run in a Web Worker with no DOM and no network, speaking only `BrainView` messages, against a published `brain-api.d.ts` they declare a version of. A script that misses a frame's deadline holds its last input, as a human holds a button; one that runs away is stopped and its runner idles. A script owns a whole seat and is never mixed into a human's input.
- **The Workshop** is a screen, reached from the menus: load a script, run it over a set of seeds, and read finish rate, times and deaths by cause, with a replay of each failure (a replay is just seed and inputs, so it's exact). `pnpm brain trial <file> --seeds N` does the same headless.
- **LLMs write brains, they don't play.** An LLM is far too slow to run every frame. Instead the Workshop's **Copy for Your AI** bundles the API, the current script and the worst failures from the last trial; the player gives that to whatever LLM they use and brings the new script back. No server, no keys, no cost on our side.
- **Open:** whether player scripts may join online rooms (marked as scripted, with a room setting to refuse them), whether bots and scripts score and can win, whether the lookahead may see moving hazards ahead of time, and whether senses name a prop's kind or only its shape and whether it hurts (Discovery).

## Platform
- Built with Next.js on Vercel. All game logic runs client-side. The server renders thin route shells and runs small, stateless signaling routes for online play.
- **Every screen is part of the game, so it's portable.** Main menu, lobby list, lobby, settings, codex, About, 404 and error screens are the game's own, in `src/`, with one look, one logo and one input system. Next is just one host. Screens are reached from the game's menus, so a build with no address bar reaches all of them; `/`, `/lobbies`, `/r/<code>`, `/codex` (and each of its cards) and `/about` are deep links too. Moving between screens never reloads the page, except leaving the error screen: Play opens a new lobby, Join checks the code first, and leaving a lobby (Leave Lobby, or Main Menu from the Kicked screen) goes back to the main menu. Debug params such as `?seed` and `?theme` stay on the URL throughout.
